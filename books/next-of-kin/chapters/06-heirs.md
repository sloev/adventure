:: c6-open
You open it at the kitchen table with a knife, because you open letters with a knife, the way Poul did, the way Aksel probably did: one clean cut along the top, never torn.

You read it twice. Then you translate it, out of habit, in the margin of the second page, in pencil:

*Nordisk Arveudredning is a Danish company which, together with international partners, traces heirs to estates where the heirs are unknown to the authorities. We have reason to believe that your mother, Karen Kjær, née Overgaard, may be entitled to a share of an estate of a deceased person abroad. As we are aware that your mother lives in a care home, we are writing to you as next of kin. A similar letter has been sent to your sister. In the interests of all parties and of the administration of the estate, we cannot give further details until an agreement has been made. Our fee is 33% of the amount paid out, and we undertake all work on documentation and contact with foreign authorities and lawyers. If there is nothing, it costs you nothing. Please note that other heirs are also being contacted and that the case is ongoing. Please return the enclosed agreement signed by 21 May 2026.*

The agreement is a page and a half of short numbered paragraphs. You read every one. *§2: The heir undertakes not to contact the estate, its administrator, the court or other heirs directly while the agreement is in force.* *§4: The fee is 33% of the gross amount distributed to the heir, plus documented costs of translation and legalisation, maximum 15,000 kr.* *§7: The agreement applies regardless of whether the heir would have become aware of the estate by other means.*

*An estate abroad.* A deceased person, unnamed.

You put the letter down on the table next to the knife.

So that's what §7 is for. For people like you, who already know. Who would sign, out of politeness, or fear, or because the letter is on cream paper with a Bredgade address, and then give a third of whatever it is to a man called Rasmus for telling them something they knew in February.

You sit there with that for a while. It's not anger, exactly. It's the feeling you get at work when you find the clause in a contract that someone was hoping you wouldn't read.

:: c6-guess
*An estate abroad.*

Abroad, for an Overgaard, from Vigsø. You've got a pretty good idea where abroad is. The airmail letter from 1962 is on the corner of your desk upstairs, next to the hymnbook. *Elk Horn, Iowa.* Harold, and the farm with 240 acres, and the girl on the porch steps with her hand up against the sun. Born in 1941. She'd be eighty-four. Eighty-five.

Or it might be someone else. Søren Peder, with his guitar, back from Buenos Aires; did he leave anyone there? Some other Overgaard who went to Canada, to New Zealand, to Australia, whom you've never found because you haven't looked. The Overgaards had a lot of children. A lot of them left.

But you think it's Iowa. You think it's Dorothy, and that she's dead, and that she never saw the sea.

You look at §7 again. *The agreement applies regardless of whether the heir would have become aware of the estate by other means.*

You read it the way you'd read a clause in a land contract that someone was hoping you'd skip. They're telling you, in plain Danish, that it can be found by other means. They're just betting you won't.

:: c6-decide
Mette comes in from the garden with a bag of weeds and finds you at the table with the three sheets spread out and the knife beside them, like the aftermath of a very small operation.

She reads it standing up, with her gardening gloves still on, holding the pages by the edges.

"Thirty-three percent," she says.

"Of something."

"Of what?"

"They won't say until I sign. That's the business. They find the heirs, and they don't tell you what they've found until you've agreed to give them a third." You tap §2. "And once I've signed, I'm not allowed to contact anyone myself. Not the court, not the lawyers, not the other heirs."

"Is it legal?"

"Completely. I looked it up last year, for a client whose uncle in Germany died. It's a whole industry. In America they're called *heir hunters*. Some of them are good. They do real work. They find people nobody else would." You turn the page over. "But they're betting that you don't know, and can't find out, and are frightened of the English, and the court, and the paperwork."

"And are you?"

You think about it honestly. A probate court in Iowa. American lawyers. Five generations of proof, in a country that has no idea what a *kirkebog* is. Your mother in a paper crown, who can't sign anything, whose guardianship application is sitting in an in-tray at Familieretshuset in a pile of three to five months. Lene, who will want it done. Bent, who will have opinions. Your own job, and Mette's new one, and Ida's exams in June.

And on the other side: three months of evenings reading church books. Every one of the people who would have to be proved is already on the wall upstairs, with a source written under them in block capitals.

"A bit," you say.

Mette peels off one glove, finger by finger. "It's your mother's," she says. "Not ours. It's hers. Whatever you do, do it as if she was standing here." She looks at the letter. "She'd have read every word, too. She read the small print on yoghurt."

On Tuesday at 10:15 Rasmus Vinther rings you at work.

He has a pleasant, educated, unhurried Copenhagen voice, and he says *tak fordi De har valgt at samarbejde med os*, thank you for choosing to work with us, and he means it, or sounds as if he does. He's careful. He confirms your name, your mother's name, her date of birth, her address at Fjordglimt. He asks whether a guardian has been appointed. You tell him it's applied for. He says *fint, det løser vi*, fine, we'll sort that out, and makes a note.

Then he tells you.

"The estate is in the United States," he says. "In the state of Iowa. The deceased is a Miss Dorothy Overgaard, of Elk Horn, Shelby County, who died on the third of February this year. She was your mother's second cousin: her grandfather, Jens Peter Overgaard, was the brother of your mother's grandfather. She died without a will and without any closer relatives. Under Iowa law, the estate passes to the descendants of her great-grandparents. She had four sets of them, and each set's descendants share equally. One set is Peder Jensen Overgaard and Maren Christensdatter, of Vigsø. That's your mother's."

You don't say anything. You're looking at your own wall, in your mind's eye, at Peder Jensen Overgaard and Maren Christensdatter in their small boxes with *Hansted sogn, ministerialbog, opslag 97* written under them in your hand.

"The estate consists mainly of farmland," says Rasmus Vinther. "Two hundred and forty acres. The administrator has had it valued. I'm not in a position to give you exact figures, and the court has not yet determined the heirs, but I can say that even after the division, we are talking about a meaningful sum. Not a fortune. But meaningful." He pauses, professionally. "Your mother's line is one of two Danish branches. We are working on the documentation for both."

"Who's the other branch?"

"I'm afraid I can't share information about other heirs."

Of course not. §2 runs both ways.

"We'll be in touch," he says. "There will be some forms. Certified copies of church-book entries for each generation, which we will obtain from Rigsarkivet. An apostille from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Proof of identity. And, when it's in place, the guardian's appointment. It takes time. American probate usually takes a year or more. But you don't need to do anything. That's the point of us."

That's the point of us. You hang up and sit at your desk. Thirty-three percent, of a considerable sum, for a phone call telling you something that was on your wall.

You don't know, yet, if you're relieved or not.

Somewhere in Iowa, a court has a file with your mother's name in it, or will soon. Somewhere in Thisted, Holger Vestergaard is pleased about a letter from Copenhagen and has no idea that his daughter married his cousin. Somewhere in Silkeborg, a woman who wrote *you're not alone* is waiting for the light. Somewhere in Sønderborg, maybe, an old man who wrote to his mother in 2009 and never got an answer is about to be told he has a brother.

And the elder is out, and Ida's exams start on Monday, and in three weeks it's Sankt Hans, and you're going to Thy.
