:: c2-open
The guide on Rigsarkivet's website is written by someone who has answered the same question ten thousand times and has decided to be patient about it.

*Start with yourself,* it says. *Work backwards one generation at a time. Never skip a generation because you "know" who came before. Write down where you found everything. The church books are the backbone of Danish genealogy: from the 1600s until 1968 every birth, baptism, confirmation, marriage and burial in the Danish state church was recorded by the parish priest, in duplicate. The censuses (folketællinger) from 1787 to 1940 tell you who lived in each household. Together they let you move from person to person, parish to parish, century to century. Most of this material is scanned and free to read on Arkivalieronline.*

*Be prepared for old handwriting.*

It's Tuesday the thirteenth, half past nine in the evening. You have been back at work for one day. Your colleagues at the firm on Søren Frichs Vej were kind in the way Danish colleagues are kind: a card on your desk signed by everyone, a box of chocolates from the partners, and nobody saying anything at all about it after ten o'clock, which was exactly right. You spent the day on a boundary dispute between two farms near Hammel and a subdivision plan for a field outside Hinnerup that will be forty-two terraced houses by 2028, and you came home and ate fish fingers with Ida because Mette was on a late, and now you're upstairs in the office with the door closed and the desk lamp on and a cup of tea going cold beside the keyboard.

You've made a page in a new notebook. A black hardback A4 notebook from the stationer's at work, squared paper, the kind you use for field sketches. On the first page, in your surveyor's block capitals, you have written:

**KILDER.** Sources. And under it, a rule you have used for twenty-five years in every land-registry search you've ever done, adapted:

*1. HVAD STÅR DER? — what does it actually say.*
*2. HVOR STÅR DET? — where, exactly: archive, book, page, image.*
*3. HVAD STÅR DER IKKE? — what it does not say.*

It looks a little pompous, written down like that. You leave it.

---

Arkivalieronline opens on a grey page with a map of Denmark and a search field. You choose *Kirkebøger*, church books, and then it asks you, in a series of dropdowns, to go down through the old administrative layers of the country like a man going down the stairs of an old house: *amt*, county; *herred*, district; *sogn*, parish. You know these words from work. Every piece of land in Denmark still sits inside a parish, and every parish inside a herred, even though nobody has used them for anything administrative since 1970.

Ringkøbing amt. Hjerm herred. Struer sogn.

A list of books appears, each with a date range and a description: *Fødte, viede, døde 1941–1953. Kontraministerialbog.* You click on it, and the screen fills with a grey photograph of a page, slightly tilted, slightly out of focus at one corner, ruled into columns by hand.

It is so ordinary, and so astonishing, that you sit back in the chair.

It's a ledger. That's all. A big ruled ledger in which a priest, or a parish clerk, wrote down each child as it was born, in blue-black ink that has gone brown, in a careful upright hand. Column after column: number, date of birth, date of baptism, name, parents, godparents, remarks. You scroll through it image by image, using the arrows. 1947. March, April, May. The ink changes colour halfway down a page where someone refilled a pen. A child born in June has a small cross in the margin and a date in July: died.

And then, on image 61, number 87 for the year:

```doc Struer sogn, kontraministerialbog 1941–1953, Fødte drenge 1947, nr. 87 (Arkivalieronline, opslag 61)
| Født | Døbt | Navn | Forældre | Faddere |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21. august 1947, Struer | 12. oktober 1947, Struer Kirke | Poul | Arbejdsmand Aksel Kjær, f. 12/5 1921 i Holstebro Landsogn, og Hustru Inger f. Bach, f. 3/11 1923 i Struer. Bopæl: Havnegade 14, Struer | Moderen bar barnet. Faddere: Arbejdsmand Niels Peter Kjær, Kjærhuset, Holstebro Landsogn; Hustru Ane Kirstine Kjær; Havnearbejder Laurits Bach, Struer |
```

*Poul.* Just *Poul*: no middle name. You knew that. It still looks very bare, on the page, a child who was given one name and kept it his whole life.

Born on the twenty-first of August 1947. Baptised in October. Inger carried him to the font. Godparents: Aksel's parents, who you never knew, and Inger's father, a harbour worker.

Aksel and Inger married in — you check your notes, from the wedding photograph in the box, if you have the box, or from memory, if you don't — 1947. Spring, you think. And Poul was born in August.

You do the sum. Then you write it in the notebook anyway, because writing a thing down is how you find out what you actually know: *Poul f. 21.8.1947. Forældre gift 1947 (hvornår?). Aksel løsladt 1947 (Bent).* Released from prison in 1947, married in 1947, son in August 1947.

So Poul was conceived — you count back on your fingers, feeling faintly ridiculous — in November 1946. When Aksel was, if Bent was right, in prison.

You sit and look at that for a while. Then you write, under question 3, *what it does not say*: *Hvornår løsladt? Fængsel med orlov? Bents "to år" — fra hvornår?* When released? Prison with leave? Bent's "two years", from when? And you underline it, and feel, for the first time, the particular itch of the thing. A boundary on the paper that doesn't match the hedge.

It's not a scandal. Plenty of couples married in a hurry in 1947. Maybe he was released early. Maybe Bent's dates are wrong. It's sixty seconds of arithmetic, on a Tuesday night, about people who are all dead.

You can't stop now. That's what you notice. Not the arithmetic: the fact that you can't stop.

---

Aksel's own baptism takes you twenty minutes to find, because Holstebro Landsogn, the rural parish around the town, turns out to have its church books bound together with Holstebro town for some years and not others, and the first book you open is the wrong one. But you find it. 1921. The handwriting has changed: a different priest, older, with a hand that leans and loops and puts curly tails on its capital letters. But you can read it. It's ordinary Latin script, just old-fashioned, and when you can't make out a word you zoom in until the screen is all brown loops and grey paper grain, and squint, and then it comes.

```doc Holstebro Landsogn, ministerialbog 1913–1925, Fødte drenge 1921, nr. 19 (Arkivalieronline, opslag 103)
| Født | Døbt | Navn | Forældre | Faddere |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12. maj 1921, Kjærhuset | 3. juli 1921, Landsogns Kirke | Aksel | Arbejdsmand Niels Peter Pedersen Kjær, f. 8/2 1893 i Holstebro Landsogn, og Hustru Ane Kirstine Jensen, f. 19/9 1897 i Ellebæk, Mejrup | Moderen bar barnet. Gaardmand Jens Ellebæk, Mejrup; Pige Mette Kirstine Pedersen, Kjærhuset; Husmand Peder Nielsen, Kjærhuset |
```

You write it out in the notebook. Then you put your finger on the screen, on the father's name, which is something you'll catch yourself doing for months.

*Niels Peter Pedersen Kjær.* Not *Kjær*: *Pedersen Kjær*. Peter's son. Of Kjærhuset. The house by the *kjær*, the marsh.

And one of the godparents is *Husmand Peder Nielsen, Kjærhuset*. A smallholder, living in the same house. Niels Peter *Peders*en; Peder *Niels*en. Father and son. The names swap and swap back down the generations, like the steps of a dance, and the family name, the name on your own passport and your father's coffin and your daughter's school bag, is not a name at all. It's an address. It's where they lived.

The guide warned you about this. *Patronymics.* Until the nineteenth century most Danes had no fixed surname: a man's son took his first name plus *-sen*, a daughter took it plus *-datter*, and they often added the name of the farm or house to tell themselves apart. *Jens Pedersen Overgaard. Peder Nielsen Kjær.* The law that made family names fixed came in 1828. In the countryside, the guide says drily, *it took several generations to be obeyed*.

You look up *kjær* in the dictionary on the shelf, because you like to be sure. *Kær, kjær, n.: low-lying wet meadow; marsh, fen.* You are a man named after a wet field.

You laugh out loud, alone, at a quarter past eleven, and then you hear yourself and stop.

On Wednesday at dinner you tell Ida about the marsh, and she laughs so much that she has to put her fork down.

"We're *swamp people*."

"Wet-meadow people."

"Swamp people. I'm telling everyone." She picks the fork up again. "Can you send me the links? The church books? I want to see."

You send them to her from your phone across the table. Mette, home from an early shift, sits at the end of the table in her hospital fleece with her feet tucked under her and watches the two of you with an expression you can't quite read. Interest, partly. And something else, further back.

"My farfar's family were Vestergaards for three generations," she says. "Before that, God knows. Probably also swamps."

"Thy hasn't got swamps. Thy's got sand."

"Sand people, then." She smiles, and gets up to put the kettle on, and puts her hand on your shoulder on the way past, and leaves it there a second longer than she needs to.

Ida is already on her laptop, at the table, with her plate pushed aside, which normally isn't allowed. Nobody says anything. She has made an account on MyHeritage. Of course she has. She has also, you see, leaning over, started a tree: *Kjær Family Tree (Ida)*, with herself at the bottom in a little green box, and you and Mette above her, and Poul and Karen and Holger and Mette's mother above you, and Aksel and Inger above Poul, with question marks for dates.

"You can add the swamp people," she says. "Niels Peter and Ane Kirstine. Look: it's free up to two hundred and fifty people. And it does this thing called Smart Matches, where it finds other people's trees with the same people in them. So someone in, like, Holstebro, who's already done the whole family, it'll just *give* you their tree. You click accept and it all comes in."

"Just like that."

"Just like that." She clicks something. A green banner slides down from the top of the screen: *Your free 14-day trial of the Complete plan has started!*

"Ida — "

"It's *free*, far. For fourteen days. Then you cancel." She looks at you with her Mette eyes. "You're going to love it."

:: c2-friday
On Friday night you read the paper in the hymnbook.

You've been not reading it for six days. You've been aware of it the whole time: the small black book on the corner of the desk, the stiffening in its pages at hymn 31, the shadow of the typescript, *…HJÆLPEN*. Each evening you've opened Arkivalieronline and gone back another generation, and each evening you've glanced at it, and not.

Then on Friday Mette is on a night shift, and Ida is at a party in Risskov with a strict instruction to text at midnight, and the house is very quiet, and at a quarter past eleven, after a long hour in the 1880 census for Holstebro Landsogn, you reach over and open the hymnbook at the place where it falls open by itself, and take out the paper, and unfold it.

It has been folded twice for so long that the creases have gone soft and furred and slightly transparent, like the creases in a map that's been carried in a pocket for years. One corner has a rust-brown mark where a paperclip was. It's a single sheet, typed on a manual typewriter with an uneven ribbon, so that some letters are darker than others, and the capital *J* sits slightly above the line every time. Printed at the top, in an old-fashioned serif, is a letterhead.

You read it once fast and once very slowly.

*Concerning your male child, born on 17 March 1942 at the Mothers' Home in Aarhus.*

*With reference to previous correspondence we hereby inform you that the Ministry of Justice on 26 June of this year granted an adoption order, by which the child has been adopted by a married couple living in South Jutland.*

*In accordance with current practice, the names of the adoptive parents and the child's new name cannot be disclosed.*

*We must at the same time draw your attention to the fact that by the order you are released from any obligation towards the child, and also no longer have any claim to it.*

*Respectfully.*

Your grandmother had a son.

In March 1942, when she was eighteen. Not with Aksel, who was, in March 1942, if Bent's story and the Frikorps pages are right, somewhere on the Eastern Front, outside a place called Demyansk, in snow. Not with anybody whose name is on this sheet, because there's no father on this sheet at all. *Frøken*: Miss. An unmarried girl of eighteen at her parents' address on Havnegade, where five years later she would be living with her husband and her new baby Poul.

She went to Aarhus to have him, to a home for unmarried mothers, and three and a half months later a typist at Mødrehjælpen with a capital *J* that jumped above the line informed her that he belonged to someone else now, somewhere in South Jutland, under a name she would not be told, and that she had no further claim to him.

And she folded it in four and put it in her confirmation hymnbook, at hymn 31, and kept it there for sixty-nine years.

You turn to hymn 31. Of course you do. You don't know the hymn; you read the first verse, and it's about God's love being like the sea, deep and wide and unending, and it doesn't help, and you close the book.

---

You sit very still. The house ticks. The radiator under the window gurgles as the night setting comes on.

Bent's mother. Poul's mother. *Min dreng*, she called you. *My boy.* Inger who baked klejner, who smelled of 4711, whom you never once saw angry except at the television news. Who sat at the kitchen table when Bent was six with a darning needle and scratched the collar off a photograph of her husband.

Poul had a brother. A half-brother. Born five years before him. You count, again, on your fingers, in the lamplight, at a quarter to midnight: 1942. He would be eighty-three. Eighty-four in March.

He could be alive.

Your phone buzzes on the desk, and you jump as if someone has touched your neck. It's Ida: *home by 1, promise, Sofie's dad is driving* and a heart, and you text back *ok* and a heart, and put the phone face down, and look at the letter in the lamplight, the furred creases and the rust mark from the paperclip, and you feel something you can only describe as vertigo. As if the floor of the office has turned out to be glass, and under it there's another floor, and another under that.

You ring Bent at ten on Saturday morning, because Bent gets up at six and will by ten have read *Holstebro Dagblad*, had two cups of coffee, walked to the baker's and back, and be at his most sociable.

"Knægt!" He's pleased. You can hear the radio behind him, P4 Midt & Vest, a phone-in about dogs. "How are you holding up?"

"Okay. Bent, I found something. In Farmor's hymnbook. Her confirmation one, from the sideboard."

"Oh yes?"

You read it to him. All of it, slowly, the Danish of 1942 with its *Angaaende* and *Ærbødigst*. At some point during the second paragraph the radio behind him goes off.

When you finish there is a silence on the line so long that you say, "Bent?"

"That's not her," he says.

"It's addressed to Frøken Inger Bach, Havnegade 14, Struer. It was in her hymnbook, Bent. With her name in it."

"There were other Bachs in Struer. It's a common name. It's — " He stops. You hear him breathing. A big man's breathing, heavy, through the nose. "She was eighteen," he says, as if that were an argument.

"Yes."

"In 1942." Another long breath. "Who was the — who was the father?"

"It doesn't say."

"No." And then, much quieter: "No. It wouldn't."

You wait. Through the window of the office you can see Mette's car turn into the drive, back from the night shift, and stop, and the engine go off, and Mette just sit there behind the wheel for a minute with her head back against the headrest, the way she does after nights.

"I've got to go, knægt," Bent says. "I've got — I'm meant to be somewhere."

"Bent — "

"Send me a picture of it. On the phone. I want to see it." A pause. "Don't tell Lene. Not yet."

He hangs up. You stand in the office with the phone in your hand. Then you photograph the letter, both sides, flat on the desk under the lamp, and send it to him, and watch the two grey ticks under it turn blue, which takes about four seconds, and then nothing, for the rest of the day, and the next, and the next.

:: c2-mette
You wait. You wait through Saturday morning while Mette sleeps off the night shift with the blackout blind down and a pillow over her head, and you make Ida pancakes, and you walk to the Netto on Viby Torv for milk and stand in the queue holding a carton of milk thinking *eighty-three, eighty-four in March*, and the cashier has to say *hej?* twice.

At half past two Mette comes downstairs in her dressing gown with her hair flattened on one side and her face creased from the pillow, and you give her coffee and wait until she has drunk half of it, and then you put the letter on the kitchen table in front of her.

She reads it with one hand round the cup. She reads it twice. She's a nurse; she has spent twenty-five years reading other people's worst news on official paper, discharge summaries and test results and letters from the municipality, and she reads it the way she reads those, steadily, to the end, with no expression at all. Then she puts the cup down and reads it again.

"Oh, Inger," she says.

That's all, for a while. She puts her hand flat on the paper as if to keep it warm.

"Did you know?" you ask, which is a stupid question.

"How would I know?" She looks up. "Did *Poul* know?"

"I don't think so. Bent doesn't know. I'm sure of that. Bent would have said, at the mindekaffe."

"Your mother?"

You think about it. You think about Karen on Sunday morning at Fjordglimt with her hands on her handbag, and you realise you have no idea at all.

"He'd be eighty-three," Mette says. She's doing the same sums you did. "Eighty-four. There are eighty-four-year-olds on my ward every week, Søren. They're not all — they're not all ninety and confused. Half of them are fitter than me." She turns the letter round, so the typed lines face you. "You're going to look for him."

It isn't a question, and it isn't quite a statement either, and you don't answer it.

"Be careful," she says. "That's all. Not for you. For him. He might not know. Or he might know and not want — " She stops. "Just be careful. People have whole lives."

She goes back upstairs to shower. You hear the water running for a long time.

:: c2-saturday
On Saturday afternoon you go back to the marsh.

It's easier than thinking about the other thing. That's the truth, and you know it while you do it. The church books are a staircase with a handrail. You go down one step at a time, and every step is solid, and at the bottom of each one there's a name.

Niels Peter Pedersen Kjær, born 8 February 1893. You find him in the 1901 census, in Kjærhuset, eight years old, with his parents Peder Nielsen, *husmand og daglejer*, smallholder and day labourer, forty-five, and Maren Christensdatter, forty-two, and four siblings, and a lodger called Ole who is listed as *fattiglem*, a pauper on parish relief, seventy-one. The census is typed out on the screen in a transcription by volunteers from Danish Family Search, neat and modern, but there's also a link to the original image, and you click it, because the guide said to, and there it is: the census taker's sheet, in pencil and ink, with Ole the pauper's name crossed out and written in again further down, as if someone had changed their mind about where he belonged.

In 1880, Peder Nielsen is twenty-four and a farmhand on a farm in Mejrup. In 1870 he is fourteen and still at home in Kjærhuset with his own parents, *Niels Pedersen* and *Mette Marie Andersdatter*. The dance again: Niels, Peder, Niels, Peder, back and back.

And Peder Nielsen's baptism, in the spring of 1856, is where the handwriting changes.

You find the right book. You find the right year. You go to the boys born in 1856, and scroll to April, and the page comes up on the screen, and you lean forward, and for the first time since Tuesday you can't read a single word.

It isn't handwriting. It's a cardiogram. It's a row of tight, spiky, slanting strokes, all the same height, linked by hairlines; letters that look like *n* and might be *u* or *e* or *r* or *c*; long sweeping descenders; an *h* that looks like a fish-hook; capital letters that are pure ornament, great curling things like the ironwork on a gate. You can make out numbers, just: the 1856, a 14, something that might be a 27. You can see where the columns are. You can tell which word is probably the name, because it's in the name column. And that is all.

*Gotisk skrift.* Gothic handwriting. The guide has a page about it, with a downloadable PDF alphabet: every letter, small and capital, in three or four variant forms, next to its modern equivalent. You print it out on the office printer. You lay it next to the keyboard. You go letter by letter, like a child with a primer, and at the end of forty minutes you have:

*P—der. Søn af Ind—te Niels P—sen og H—tru M—te M—ie And—sd—r i Kj—huset.*

Peder. Son of — something — Niels Pedersen and wife Mette Marie Andersdatter in Kjærhuset.

It's something. It's maybe two-thirds. The missing third is the bit you'd most like to have: the word before *Niels*, which is his occupation or status, and the godparents, which are four lines of fish-hooks, and a remark in the right-hand margin, written smaller, at an angle, that might be about anything at all.

It's nearly six. Downstairs Mette is making dinner. You can hear the extractor fan and the radio. You lean back and press the heels of your hands into your eyes.

Twelve hundred kroner. You do the sum you always do with money, which is in hours: about three hours of your time billed to a client, or a week's food for the household, or one night in a hotel in Copenhagen in high season. It's not nothing. It's not a lot. It's a subscription to a website that keeps telling you, with confetti, that you have discovered your family.

What you've actually discovered in fourteen days has almost all come from the free scans on Arkivalieronline, read by your own eyes, or Birthe's, or a machine's. MyHeritage has given you a tree to put it in, and a page of Smart Matches, some of them wrong, and the promise of records in other countries. Iowa, for instance. On the last Saturday of January, the thirty-first, it snows for the first time all winter, a thin wet snow that settles on the cars and the hedges and not on the road, and you take Ida to her handball match in Tilst and stand on the side with the other parents in a sports hall that smells of rubber and floor polish, and you are, for an hour and a half, not thinking about the dead at all. Her team loses by four. She scores twice. On the drive home she is furious about the referee, and you agree with everything she says, and it's the best hour you've had in a month.

That evening, the office. The wall. The tree, on its sheet of A3, which you redraw from scratch with a ruler and a fine black drawing pen from work, because the first version was getting messy: up to 1787 on the Overgaard side, fisherman and beach bailiff and cottager on the dunes; to 1856, or to 1690, depending on what you believed on the twentieth, on the Kjær side. Small boxes. Block capitals. One name per box, and under it, in smaller writing, *where it says so*.

Two lines that go off to the side and end in a question mark. One from Inger, in 1942. One from Ane Christensdatter, in 1798.

And on the Kjær side, at the bottom, your father: *POUL KJÆR, 21.8.1947 – 2.1.2026*. And next to him, Karen. And below them you, and Lene, and below you Ida and Magnus.

You stand back and look at it, the way you'd stand back from a finished survey drawing, checking the lines meet. They do. Everything joins up. Every box has a source written under it.

The only box on the whole sheet without a source is yours.

You haven't looked yourself up. It didn't occur to you. You're the one person on the wall you don't need a church book for: you know when you were born, and where, and to whom. *Søren Poul Kjær, 14.3.1977, Holstebro. Søn af Poul Kjær og Karen Overgaard.* You know it the way you know your own phone number. There's nothing to look up.

On the hall-cupboard shelf, downstairs, above the coats, is a small white box with a green logo, still in its cellophane.

Ida's results, she tells you at breakfast on Sunday, refreshing the app for the fourth time that morning, have changed from *Received at lab* to *DNA extraction in progress*. Four to six weeks, it says. Maybe sooner.
