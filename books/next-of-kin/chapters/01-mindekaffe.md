:: c1-thursday
The key is on a ring with a plastic fob from the Struer Boldklub, worn smooth and white at the edges, and it sticks in the lock the way it has stuck for thirty years. You have to lift the door slightly by the handle and turn at the same time. Your hand remembers before you do.

It is twenty past six on Thursday evening and fully dark. The wind comes in off the fjord, along the railway line and between the terraced houses, and it smells of salt and diesel and somebody's wood-burning stove. The carport is empty. The Toyota went to Lene's husband on Monday, because Lene said someone had to move it before the insurance ran out and Jesper had a trailer hitch, and you said fine, because you didn't know what else to say. The space where it stood has a dark rectangle on the concrete where the rain hasn't reached.

Inside, the house smells of him.

It is not a smell you could describe to anyone. Coffee, a little. The soap he bought in multipacks from Rema 1000. Machine oil, faintly, always, even after eleven years of retirement, as if B&O had got into his skin in 1966 and stayed there. Under it the smell of a house where the heating has been turned down to fifteen degrees because nobody lives in it.

You turn the radiator in the hall up to three, then think about the heating bill and who will pay it and turn it back to two.

You are here for a photograph.

That is the whole errand, and you said it out loud to Mette on the phone from the car park at Gødstrup on Tuesday, after the meeting with the undertaker: *I just need to find a good picture of him for the table.* At a mindekaffe there is always a picture on a small table by the door, next to a candle and the guest book, and people stop in front of it on their way in with their coats still on, and say *ja*, and nod. The undertaker, a soft-spoken man called Knudsen with very clean hands, had asked whether the family had a photograph they would like enlarged and framed. *Something where he looks like himself,* he said. *People always want one where he looks like himself.*

Lene said she'd find one. Then Lene rang on Wednesday and said she hadn't had time, with the kids and work, and could you, since you were driving over anyway on Thursday to collect the suit.

The suit is the other errand. It's hanging in the bedroom wardrobe in a dry-cleaner's bag from 2019, from your cousin Tina's wedding: dark grey, single-breasted, too wide in the shoulders now. Poul will not be wearing it. He'll be cremated in what the undertaker called "his own clothes, if you like," and you and Lene chose the blue checked shirt and the good trousers and the cardigan with the leather buttons that Karen knitted in about 1990, because that's how you see him. But Knudsen said people often like to put a folded garment in the coffin, something with meaning, and Lene said the suit, he always looked so smart in the suit, and you didn't argue.

You stand in the hall with your coat on.

On the little table by the phone there is a pile of post: a letter from Struer Forsyning, a Bilka leaflet, a bank statement, a card from the Danske Bank branch in Holstebro offering condolences, which was quick of them. His reading glasses are on top, folded. Next to them, the cordless phone in its cradle, its little green light blinking: a message. You don't play it.

His slippers are under the coat rack, side by side, heels together.

You knew they would be. You still have to stand there for a while.

---

Poul Kjær died on Friday the second of January at 14:40 in a bed on the fourth floor of the new hospital at Gødstrup, which is so large and so clean and so far from anywhere that the nurses give you a map. He had a stroke on New Year's Eve, at home, some time after the New Year's speech at six. *The Queen's speech*, he'd have called it. *The King's*, Lene corrected you on the phone, when you said it like that, and then started crying, because he'd always said *the Queen* too and refused to learn. He was watching on his own. He'd said he didn't want to come to Odense, it was too far and the fireworks frightened Lene's dog and he'd rather sit at home with a beer and *Matador* on DR2. A neighbour, Birgit from number 14, found him at half past twelve, when she came round to wish him a happy new year with a glass of champagne. He was on the floor of the living room with the television still on. Nobody knows how long he'd been lying there. The doctors said it didn't change anything. You've decided to believe them. He'd managed to pull the cloth off the coffee table and everything on it had fallen with him. Birgit told you about the cloth twice, as if it mattered. Maybe it does.

You drove over at four in the morning on New Year's Day, through fog on the motorway to Herning and black empty roads beyond, with Mette in the passenger seat asleep against the window. He was awake when you got there, in the way that people are awake after a stroke: the eyes open, the face pulled down on the right side, the mouth working. He knew you. You're nearly certain he knew you. He squeezed your hand with his left hand and made a sound that could have been *knægt*, which was what Bent called you, which Poul never did, which is why you think you misheard. On the second day he stopped squeezing.

The doctor who talked to you, a young woman with a Swedish accent and very tired eyes, said *massive*, and *the left hemisphere*, and *comfort*, and you nodded at all of it.

Lene arrived on the second from Odense in time. Karen didn't come; the plejehjem said she was having a difficult week, and you and Lene agreed in a corridor that it would only frighten her. You still don't know if that was right.

He was seventy-eight.

---

The photographs are in the bedroom.

You know where, roughly. There are albums in the sideboard in the living room, the big ones with the cellophane pages that Karen kept until about 1995: your christening, Lene's christening, holidays at Blokhus and one at Lake Garda, every Christmas with the tree in the same corner and the same star. But those are Karen's albums. They're pictures of the family. You want a picture of him.

And Poul kept his own things in the bedroom wardrobe, on the shelf above the clothes rail, where his father kept things, where he'd told you once, years ago, the important papers were, "in case." Life insurance. The deeds to the house. The papers for the Toyota. A folder from the union with his pension. He'd shown it to you after Karen went into the plejehjem, standing on a chair, the chair creaking, him saying *it's all here, Søren, you won't have to look for anything,* in the voice he used for things he didn't want to talk about.

You go in and switch on the light.

The bed is made. He always made the bed. The duvet is pulled flat and the two pillows are side by side, though for two years only one has been used. Karen's side of the wardrobe still has her clothes in it; you can see the sleeve of her red winter coat.

You find the suit, in its plastic, and lay it over the bed. Then you get the chair from the kitchen and stand on it and look at the shelf.

It is exactly as he showed you: a blue plastic document folder with a press-stud, a grey ring binder labelled *HUS* in his square toolmaker's capitals, another labelled *BIL*, a smaller one labelled *PENSION / FORSIKRING*. A shoebox for a pair of Ecco shoes, size 44, with the lid held on by two thick rubber bands that have gone hard and brittle with age. And at the back, against the wall, an old wooden cigar box that you have never seen before.

You take down the shoebox first, because it's in front.

The rubber bands snap when you touch them, both of them, *tak*, *tak*, like small dry twigs.

---

Inside are photographs. Not in albums, not in order: just photographs, a few hundred of them, loose and in envelopes, black-and-white and the brown-orange colour of the seventies and the overexposed white of the early digital years printed at Fotokemi. You sit on the edge of the bed with the box on your knees and go through them, and you lose about forty minutes, though you'll only notice that later, when you look at your phone.

Poul at about seventeen, in overalls, in front of a lathe, grinning with half his teeth. On the back, in pencil, in someone else's handwriting: *Poul, B&O, 1. lærlingeår, 1963.*

Poul and Bent as boys, on a jetty, in swimming trunks, thin as matchsticks, squinting. Bent has his arm round Poul's neck in a way that is half hug and half headlock.

Poul and Karen at a dance, she in a dress with a full skirt, he in a narrow tie, both looking at whoever is holding the camera as if they've been caught.

Poul holding a baby: you. On the back: *Søren 3 uger.* Karen's writing. He's holding you out a little from his body, carefully, with both hands under you, the way you'd hold a piece of work you'd just finished and weren't sure was right.

A man you don't recognise, in a hat, in front of a white wooden farmhouse with a porch and a car with fins. Then you turn it over: *Elk Horn, Iowa 1962. Harold og Dorothy.* There is a girl of about twenty on the porch steps, in a light-coloured dress, with her hand up against the sun.

Folded with it, in its envelope, is a letter: an airmail envelope, very thin, blue, with red and blue chevrons around the border, a six-cent stamp with an eagle, and a postmark: ELK HORN IOWA JUL 3 1962. It's addressed in looping handwriting to *Mr. Niels Overgaard, Vigsø, Thisted, Denmark.* Your mother's father. Your morfar, who died when you were seven, who you remember mainly as a pair of enormous hands and a smell of tobacco and fish.

You don't read it yet. You put it on the bed, on the suit.

Then, near the bottom of the box, in a brown envelope with no writing on it, there is a photograph that you look at for a long time.

It is a studio portrait, postcard size, with a photographer's stamp on the back in German: *Foto-Atelier* something, an address in a town whose name has been rubbed half away. A young man. Eighteen, nineteen, twenty. Head and shoulders, three-quarters to the camera, in a uniform: a dark tunic, buttoned to the neck, with an open collar showing a lighter shirt beneath; a cap with a peak and a badge on it, tilted slightly. He is very serious, the way young men in old photographs are serious, as though the camera were a test. He has your father's ears. He has, you realise with a small electric shock, your own chin.

It's your grandfather. It's Aksel. You've seen him old, at Christmas, with a cigar and a laugh like a car starting; you've seen him in the wedding photograph with Inger, in 1947, in a borrowed suit. You've never seen him like this.

On the right collar tab there was something. You can see where it was. It has been scratched away, carefully, thoroughly, with something sharp, a pin or the point of a compass, until the emulsion is gone and there is only a small square of white paper fibre where the patch on the collar used to be. The badge on the cap has been scratched too, but less completely, as if whoever did it lost heart. On the left sleeve, just below the shoulder, there's a small dark shield with a lighter cross on it. You hold it under the bedside lamp and tilt it. It could be a Danish flag. On a German uniform. That makes no sense.

The family story is that Farfar was in Germany during the war. Bent tells it. *Far was down in Germany in the war, and after the war they put him in a camp for it, and he never talked about it.* You've heard it at Christmas tables since you were small, in the same tone as stories about the bad winter of 1947 or the time the ferry at Oddesund ran aground. Germany, a camp, never talked about it. You always assumed — you don't know what you assumed. Forced labour, maybe. Some of them were sent to work in German factories, weren't they. Or that he'd been a sailor. Or that he was in a camp the way people were in camps: as a prisoner of the Germans.

You never asked. That's the thing you feel now, sitting on your parents' bed with the photograph in your hand and the wind against the window: not shock exactly, but the old embarrassment of a question you should have asked forty years ago and never did, because it was never the moment, because there were always cousins and cake, because Farfar laughed his car-starting laugh and gave you a fifty-kroner note and told you not to tell your mother.

He died in 1989. You were twelve.

The cigar box, when you take it down, contains nothing but a pocket watch that doesn't work, an enamel pin from the Struer angling club, three keys to nothing, and a fold of Danish banknotes from the 1970s with Hans Christian Ørsted on them, worth nothing either.

You slide it into the inside pocket of your coat, which is hanging on the back of the bedroom door, the way you'd put away a receipt you might need. Then you take it out again, because you think it might get bent, and put it inside the airmail envelope with Harold's letter, which is stiffer, and decide that the envelope will go in your pocket instead.

Bent will be at the church. Bent will be at the mindekaffe. Bent will have had two glasses of red wine by three o'clock and will be talking to everyone at once, and there will be a moment, you think, when you can take him by the elbow and say *Bent, I found this, who took it?* and he'll look, and tell you. Bent loves to tell. Bent is the family's storyteller. It's only that you've never asked him for this story.

:: c1-shelf
You put it back in its brown envelope and the envelope back near the bottom of the box, under Poul and Karen at the dance and Søren three weeks old. Then you sit a moment with your hand flat on the lid.

Not this week. This week is for him. Whatever Aksel did or didn't do in a German uniform, he has been dead for thirty-seven years and will still be dead in February. You can ask Bent in February. You can ask Bent at Easter. Bent isn't going anywhere.

You think that, and then you think about how long you thought Poul wasn't going anywhere, and you push the thought away, because there is a photograph to choose.

:: c1-picture
In the end you choose one from the shoebox: Poul at about sixty, on the bench outside the back door, in the cardigan with the leather buttons, with a cup of coffee in one hand and the other hand raised, palm out, as if to stop whoever's holding the camera, because he hated having his picture taken. He's laughing. It's the summer of 2008; the grass behind him is high and yellow. You think Lene took it, or maybe you did, on the summer holiday when Magnus was three and Ida was a baby and you all came for a week and slept on the floor.

He looks like himself.

You put it in the folder with the suit. The airmail letter you put in your coat pocket, without being entirely sure why, except that it's addressed to your morfar and it's the only thing in the box that came from very far away.

Before you leave, you play the message on the answering machine, because you can't not.

It's from the dentist in Struer, a recorded voice, reminding Poul Kjær of his appointment on Thursday the eighth of January at 14:15, and asking him to call if he cannot attend.

You sit down on the chair in the hall, under the coats, next to his slippers, and stay there for a while.

Then you turn off the lights, lift the front door by the handle, turn the key, and drive back to Lene's husband's cousin's holiday flat in Humlum, where you're all staying, with the suit on the back seat and the wind shoving at the car on the bridge.

:: c1-friday
Struer Kirke is a red-brick church on a slope above the town, with a squat tower and a churchyard that runs down towards the fjord in terraces, and on Friday the ninth of January at 11:00 it is about two-thirds full, which Bent will later say, several times, is very good for a January.

It's three degrees and blowing. The flag on the pole by the gate is at half-mast and cracking like a whip. People come up the path in dark coats with their heads down, holding their hats, and stop inside the porch to stamp their feet and unwind their scarves and say *nå* to each other in low voices. You stand just inside the door with Lene and shake hands. You know perhaps half of them. Neighbours from the street. Men from B&O, retired, in their seventies and eighties, with big hands and hearing aids, who tell you their names and the department they were in, *værktøjet* or *presseriet* or *lakeringen*, as if that's the essential information, and maybe it is. Birgit from number 14 in a fur hat, who holds your hand in both of hers and says *I found him, you know* in a whisper, as if you might not know. Two women from Karen's old knitting group. The man who ran the Spar on the corner until it closed. Poul's doctor. The young couple who bought the house opposite in 2022 and who, you realise, came because Poul helped them fix their gutter.

Mette stands one step behind you and takes the coats nobody knows where to put. Ida and Magnus are in the second row, Ida in a black dress that's too thin for January, with your old grey cardigan over it, Magnus in the suit he bought for his gymnasium graduation, which is too short in the arms now. He has had a haircut. You notice that and something in your chest goes soft and painful at once.

Then the plejehjem's minibus pulls up at the gate, and a carer in a padded jacket gets out and unfolds a wheelchair from the back, and lifts your mother into it with a practised turn, and pushes her up the path against the wind.

Karen is wearing her good navy coat and a hat you haven't seen before, and someone has done her hair. She looks small and very upright, with her handbag on her lap held in both hands, the way she held it on the bus to Holstebro when you were a child. The carer, Dorthe, a broad woman of about sixty with a West Jutland accent so thick it's almost a song, wheels her right to you.

"Here's your son, Karen," Dorthe says.

Karen looks up at you. There is a moment where you can see her searching, the way you search for a word on the tip of your tongue, and then her face clears.

"Poul," she says. "You've put on your good coat."

"It's Søren, Mor."

"I know who you are," she says, a little sharply, and pats your hand, and looks past you into the church, at the white coffin in front of the altar under its blanket of flowers, and says nothing at all.

---

Pastor Hanne Riis is in her fifties, with short grey hair and reading glasses on a cord, and she came to the house on Tuesday to talk to you and Lene about Poul, sitting on his sofa with a cup of his coffee, writing in a small notebook. She asked good questions. What did he like. What did he make. What did he say when he was proud of you. You found you had no answer to the last one, and Lene said *he didn't say anything, he went out to the shed*, and the pastor nodded as if that were a perfectly good answer, and wrote it down.

Now she stands at the front and speaks about him for nine minutes, and she has put it together well: the apprenticeship at B&O at fifteen, the thirty-eight years, the steady hands; the man who fixed things for neighbours and wouldn't take money for it; the father who drove to Aarhus with a trailer every time his son moved flat; the grandfather who taught Ida to use a file and a vice when she was eight, which Ida told her about on the phone, which you didn't know. *He went out to the shed*, she says, and a few people laugh, softly, the good kind of laughter, and Lene puts her face in her hands.

You sing "Altid frejdig, når du går", because it's always "Altid frejdig", and "Lysets engel går med glans", because Karen chose it, in 2023, on a good day, when you were still asking her things like that. The organist plays slowly. The men from B&O sing in deep, uneven voices a bar behind everyone else.

And your mother sings.

She sings every word of every verse, without a hymnbook, in a clear, thin, entirely accurate soprano. She doesn't know what year it is. She thought you were your father an hour ago. She sings *Lysets engel går med glans / gennem himlens sale* as if she has been rehearsing it all week, and when the hymn ends she folds her hands on her handbag again and looks at the coffin with an expression of calm, polite interest, as if she were at a concert.

Dorthe catches your eye across the aisle and gives a little nod. *That's how it is*, the nod says. *The songs are the last thing to go.*

---

You carry the coffin out with Bent, Jesper, Magnus, Lene's son Oliver, who is nineteen and very pale, and Poul's old foreman from B&O, a man called Erling who must be eighty and who insisted. It's heavier than you expect. It always is, people say. The pastor goes ahead with the cross. The bell begins. The wind takes the flowers on top of the coffin and you all lean into it together, the six of you, down the path to the black car at the gate, and slide him in, and step back.

There's no grave today. Poul wanted to be cremated, which surprised nobody, and the urn will be interred in the spring, next to the place where Karen will one day lie, in the corner of the churchyard by the beech hedge where his parents are. *Urnenedsættelse*, the undertaker called it, and gave you a leaflet.

The car drives away down the hill towards the crematorium at Holstebro, and the bell stops, and everyone stands in the wind not knowing what to do with their hands.

Then Bent, who is seventy-four and broad and red-faced and wearing a black tie with a tiny Struer Boldklub crest on it, claps his hands once and says, loudly, to everyone: "Nå. Der er kaffe på Havnehotellet."

There's coffee at the harbour hotel.

:: c1-mindekaffe
The Havnehotel is a long yellow building facing the harbour, with a function room on the first floor that has hosted, at a guess, every confirmation, silver wedding and mindekaffe in Struer since 1970. Long tables with white cloths. Thermos jugs of coffee every metre, the old steel ones with the push-button tops. Plates of sandwiches, the small open kind, *smørrebrød* cut into squares: egg and prawns, roast beef with remoulade and crispy onions, liver pâté with beetroot, cheese with a slice of pepper. Then layer cake. Then *kringle*. Then more coffee. There are bottles of red wine and white on each table and nobody touches them until Bent does, which is about twelve minutes after he arrives.

Your photograph of Poul stands on a little table by the door, framed, next to a lit candle and the guest book. The undertaker had it enlarged. Poul's raised hand is the size of a real hand. People stop in front of it in their coats and say *ja*, and nod, just as Knudsen said they would. One of the B&O men laughs out loud and says *that's him, that's Poul, he'd never let you take his picture*, and dabs his eyes with a handkerchief the size of a tea towel.

Karen doesn't come to the hotel. Dorthe says the church was enough for today, and takes her back to the plejehjem in the minibus, and Karen waves from the window, a small queenly wave, at nobody in particular.

You sit at the top table with Mette, Lene, Jesper and Bent. Ida and Magnus sit at the far end with Lene's two, Oliver and Freja, sixteen, and you can see the four of them leaning in together over Freja's phone and you are briefly, irrationally grateful to whatever it is they're watching.

Bent speaks after the layer cake.

He taps his glass with a teaspoon and stands up, and pushes his reading glasses up on his forehead, and doesn't use the piece of paper he has in his hand. He's good at this. He's done it at every family occasion you can remember: christenings, weddings, his own wife Grethe's funeral in 2019. He talks about Poul as a boy: the two of them on the jetty, Poul always the careful one, Bent always the one in trouble. About Poul's first moped. About the time Poul took apart the family radio to see how it worked and couldn't get it back together and their father made him sleep in the shed. People laugh. About B&O, and how proud Poul was when he made the press tool for the aluminium front of some famous record player, Bent can't remember which, the one in the museum.

"And our father," Bent says, and his voice changes a little, drops, gets rougher, "our father, Aksel, wasn't an easy man. He'd been through things. He'd been down in Germany in the war, as some of you will remember, and after the war they put him in a camp for it, and he never talked about it. Not once. But he taught Poul to work with his hands, and he taught him to be decent, and to keep his mouth shut and get on with it. And that's what Poul did. His whole life. He got on with it." He raises his glass. "Poul."

"Poul," says the room, and drinks coffee, or wine, or Faxe Kondi.

Bent sits down heavily next to you and wipes his eyes with the back of his hand and says, to nobody: "That was all right, wasn't it."

"That was good, Bent."

"He'd have hated it." He reaches for the wine.

---

The mindekaffe goes on the way they do. People move between tables with their cups. The noise rises. Somebody's grandchild crawls under the top table. Lene goes round with the thermos jug, because Lene can't sit still, and keeps glancing at you as if she wants to say something and is waiting for the right moment. At the far end, Ida has got up and is standing at the window with her arms folded, looking out at the harbour, at the masts of the moored boats rocking in the wind.

Bent, beside you, is on his third glass and telling Erling the foreman about his hip.

"Bent. Have you got a minute?"

He looks at you over the glass. "For you, knægt, I've got all day."

You take him out onto the landing at the top of the stairs, where there's a window over the car park and a rack of coats and a radiator that clanks, and it's quieter. You take the airmail envelope out of your inside pocket and the photograph out of the envelope, and hand it to him.

He holds it at arm's length, then pulls his reading glasses down from his forehead and looks again.

For a long moment he doesn't say anything at all. The noise of the mindekaffe comes through the door behind you, cups and laughter and the scrape of a chair. Bent's face doesn't do anything you can read. He's a big man, and he's gone very still, the way big men do when they're deciding something.

"Where'd you find that?"

"In the wardrobe. A shoebox, on the shelf. With Far's papers."

"Hm." He turns it over, reads the faded photographer's stamp, turns it back. He touches the scratched collar with the nail of his thumb, very lightly. "Mor did that," he says. "Inger. I remember her doing it. I must have been, what. Six. Seven. At the kitchen table, with a darning needle. I asked her what she was doing and she said she was cleaning it." He breathes out through his nose. "Cleaning it."

"What was it? On the collar?"

He looks at you then, properly, and you feel about twelve.

"He was in the Frikorps," Bent says. Quietly, though there's nobody on the landing but you. "Frikorps Danmark. He volunteered. In '41. He went to fight the Russians. A lot of young lads did, Søren, more than people want to remember now. There was no work. The Russians had gone into Finland. People thought — " He stops. "He was twenty. He was wounded out there, in the shoulder, you'll remember the scar. He came home. After the war they sent him to prison for it, two years, and he did his time, and he came out, and he married Mor, and he never said one word about it in his life. Not to me. Not to Poul. I know what I know from Mor, and from what people said in the town, and from the scar."

"I didn't know."

"No. Well." He hands the photograph back to you. His hand isn't quite steady, but that could be the wine. "Now you know. And I'll tell you what I told Poul when he asked, in about 1975: he was our father, and he was a good father, and what he did when he was twenty, he paid for. That's it. That's all there is." He puts his glasses back up on his forehead. "Put it away, knægt. Not today. Don't go telling it at the table."

He goes back in through the door, and the noise swells and closes behind him, and you hear him say something loud and cheerful to someone, and somebody laughs.

You stand on the landing and look at the young man with your chin.

*Frikorps Danmark.* You have heard the words before, the way you've heard of the Schalburg Corps and the HIPO, as items in a history lesson at gymnasium in 1994 that you didn't listen to very carefully: Danes who volunteered for the German side. Traitors, the textbook said, or did it? You can't remember. You have never once connected those words to anyone who gave you fifty kroner at Christmas.

You put the photograph back in the envelope, and the envelope back in your pocket. Your hand stays on it for a while from the outside, through the cloth, as if it might get out.

:: c1-lene
Lene is at the coffee station by the wall, refilling a thermos from the big urn, and she doesn't look up when you come over.

"You've been looking at me for half an hour," you say.

"I have not."

"Lene."

She puts the thermos down. She's forty-five and she looks like Karen did at forty-five, which you only notice at moments like this: the same small, sharp face, the same way of pressing her lips together when she's about to say something she's rehearsed. She's a school secretary in Odense, at a big school in Bolbro with nine hundred pupils, and she runs it, everybody says, better than the headmaster does. She runs everything better. She has had a spreadsheet for the funeral since the second of January. Her mascara has run a little at one corner and she hasn't noticed.

"The house," she says.

"Okay."

"I talked to the skifteret on Wednesday. The probate court. The woman was very nice. Because Mor's alive, and they owned it together, it's not just ours, it's hers, mostly, and because Mor can't sign anything, nobody can sell anything or do anything until there's a værge for her.
