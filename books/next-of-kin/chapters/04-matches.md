Ida sits on the end of the bed, between your feet and Mette's, in her pyjamas, with the phone held so you can both see, and the app opens on an animation: a globe, turning, slowing, settling on northern Europe, and then coloured shapes blooming across it like ink in water. :: c4-leeds
Mette's results come in on Friday the sixth. She's at work; she texts you a screenshot from the hospital canteen, with no comment except a single *!*. Ida, it says, at the top: *Daughter. 3,478 cM (48.4%)*. Then Jonas and Rikke and Mette's brother's other son and her mother's cousins in Hjørring and Thisted, and a long tail of Vestergaards and Kristensens and Thy names. No Camilla Bruun. No Morten Skov. No Pia H.

So they're not on Mette's side. So they're on Ida's other side. Yours.

You knew that. You've known it since Saturday morning on the end of the bed. You've been not-knowing it all week the way you can not-know where your keys are while looking straight at them.

---

On Sunday the eighth Ida takes over the kitchen table.

She's watched videos. She has a method. It's called the Leeds Method, after the American genealogist who came up with it, and it is, she explains, very simple, which is what people always say about methods just before they become complicated. You take your list of matches between about ninety and four hundred centimorgans: second and third cousins, roughly. You give each one a colour, one at a time, according to which other matches they also match. Everyone who shares DNA with each other gets the same colour. And because every person has four grandparents, the matches should fall, more or less, into four colour groups. Four clusters. One for each grandparent.

"Then you look at who's in each cluster, and their trees, and you work out which grandparent it is," Ida says. "If one cluster's all Thy people called Overgaard, that's Farmor. If one's all Vestergaards, that's Morfar. Easy."

She has done it on a spreadsheet, of course, but she's also printed it out, because you're her father and you like paper, and she's coloured it with highlighter pens: yellow, green, pink, blue. The printout covers half the table. Mette brings coffee and sits down at the end with her own cup, and looks at it with the expression she uses for other people's X-rays.

It works. That's the first astonishing thing. It *works*. The colours fall into four groups, as if somebody had sorted them by hand.

*Yellow:* thirty-one matches, Vestergaards and Holms and Thy names, Thisted and Hanstholm and Vestervig. "Morfar," says Ida. "Holger. Your dad, Mor."

*Green:* twenty-eight matches, Kristensens and Jensens, Hjørring and Frederikshavn and Sæby, the far north, the tip of Jutland. "Mormor. Bodil. Your mum." Mette nods.

*Pink:* forty-four matches. Overgaards. Thomsens. Christensens from Klitmøller. Niels Thomsen Overgaard at the top. "Farmor. Karen. Thy as well, but different Thy. Vigsø, Hanstholm." Ida taps a few names. "Some of these are in Mor's yellow group too, a little bit. Look. These three. They match Mor *and* you, far, I mean they match me on both sides. It's because it's all Thy. Everyone in Thy is related to everyone in Thy." She says it cheerfully, a fact about Thy, and moves on, and you see Mette's eyes stay on those three names a second longer.

And *blue*.

Blue should be Poul. Blue should be the Kjærs of Kjærhuset and the Bachs of Struer harbour, Bent's grandsons and their cousins and the descendants of Anders Nielsen Kjær, godparent in 1856, and Peder Nielsen of the marsh, and the farmhands of Mejrup and Ellebæk.

Blue is nineteen matches. And when Ida looks at their trees, those who have trees, and their locations, those who show them, they are from Holstebro, from Struer, from Lemvig, from Vinderup, Herning, Ringkøbing, Skive. West Jutland. Poul's country. Not one of them has a tree with a Kjær in it, or a Bach. Not one of them matches Kenneth's boys, because Kenneth's boys aren't there. Most have no tree at all, or a tree of three or four people with the names hidden. Several are listed with only initials. And at the top of the blue group, above all of them, the three women and men at *half aunt* level, Camilla Bruun, Morten Skov, Pia H., who all match each other, it turns out, at a level that MyHeritage calls *half sibling*.

"They're half siblings," says Ida. "Camilla, Morten and Pia. Of each other. I checked. They match each other at like seventeen hundred. They're all born in the seventies, it says on two of them." She puts the blue highlighter down. "And they're my half aunts. All three. Or something like it."

The coffee machine clicks as it switches itself off.

"So either," says Ida, slowly, the way she does a maths problem out loud, "farfar isn't — farfar isn't farfar. Like, biologically. And these are his — " she stops. "No, wait, that doesn't work, because then they'd be farfar's kids and they'd be your half-siblings, far, and my half aunts, and that's — that *does* work, actually. Or." She stops again. She doesn't finish.

Mette puts her cup down, very quietly.

"Or what?" you say.

Ida looks at you. She has a streak of blue highlighter on the side of her finger. "Or nothing," she says. "I don't know. It's complicated. There's loads of explanations. Adoptions. Mix-ups. Maybe the boys just didn't test." She gathers up the printout. "I'm going to do more of the blue ones tomorrow. Some of them have bigger trees. Maybe I've done it wrong."

She hasn't done it wrong. You know that, and she knows that, and Mette knows that.

She takes the printout upstairs with her. You hear her door close.

You read it the way you'd read a survey report from someone else's firm: line by line, top to bottom, checking each figure against what you'd expect, and noting the ones that don't fit.

Ida, at the top. *Daughter.* Of course. That's the one thing you never thought to worry about, and it's there, and it's right, and you feel a relief so sharp it's almost painful, and then feel ashamed of having felt it.

Then: *Half sibling.* *Half sibling.* *Half sibling.*

Pia H., Lemvig. Camilla Bruun, Silkeborg. Morten Skov, Herning.

Not *half aunt*, now. Not *1st cousin*. On your list, they're one step closer, as they would be. One step up from Ida. *Half sibling*, each one, at around seventeen or eighteen hundred centimorgans, a quarter of your DNA, the amount you share with someone with whom you share one parent.

You do not have three half-siblings. You have one sister. Lene. Born 1980, in Holstebro, to Poul and Karen Kjær.

Lene isn't on the list, because Lene said no.

No Kjær. You scroll anyway, the way you'd walk a boundary twice. Two thousand and ninety-four names. Overgaards, Thomsens, Christensens, Klitmøller, Hanstholm, Vigsø, Thisted: your mother's people, dozens of them, exactly where they should be, at exactly the levels they should be, *2nd cousin*, *3rd cousin*, the descendants of Peder Jensen Overgaard and Maren Christensdatter and the strandfoged and the cottager in the dunes, all present and correct. And on the other side: nothing. No Kjær. No Bach. Not one descendant of Peder Nielsen of the marsh, or of Laurits Bach of Struer harbour. Just a cluster of strangers from West Jutland, Lemvig, Herning, Struer, Holstebro, with no trees, or tiny trees, or initials. And three of them at the top. Half siblings.

There's a word for this. You've read it, in the last weeks, in the DNA groups, without paying attention, the way you read the warnings on medicine packets. *NPE.* *Not Parent Expected.* Also, more gently, in the Danish group: *uventet forældreskab*, unexpected parentage. *A DNA result that shows that one of your parents is not the person you thought.*

You sit in the car in Hinnerup with the windscreen fogged solid and the heater roaring, and the phone in your hand, and you think about Poul's box in the kitchen drawer, under the dishwasher guarantee, in its perfect cellophane.

*He read the manual for the toaster.*

He knew. Of course he knew. That's what the box was. He didn't forget it. He didn't not understand computers. He knew exactly what would happen when Ida sent hers in, and yours, and his own; he knew that his own DNA and yours would not match, and that a list on a screen would say so, to his granddaughter, without asking him. So he put it under the dishwasher guarantee and closed the drawer, and said *after New Year*, and then he died.

And then you see the last line.

*Mette Kjær. 3rd – 4th cousin. 151 cM.*

You read it three times. You think, for a moment, that it's a bug. That the app has put your wife on your list because you share an account, or an address, or a surname. Then you see the segments, *9 shared segments, largest 33 cM*, and you think about Thy, about Ida's three pink names that were also yellow, about the Post-it on your wall that says *Ane Marie 1884 — samme?*, and you start, sitting there alone in a fogged-up Škoda in a car park in Hinnerup, to laugh.

You laugh until it isn't laughing.

Then you wipe your face, and the windscreen, with the same sleeve, and drive home.

You turn forty-nine on Saturday the fourteenth of March, and the house fills up.

It's a Danish birthday, which means flags: a small paper Dannebrog on a stick in every slice of the layer cake, a string of little cloth flags along the kitchen window, the big flag on the pole in the garden that you put up yourself at eight in the morning in the drizzle because Mette said *your father always put it up for you*, which is true, and which you'd forgotten. It means singing *I dag er det Søren's fødselsdag, hurra, hurra, hurra* at the table, and everybody pointing at you on the *hurra*s. It means a layer cake from the baker on Viby Torv and a home-made one from Mette, and *boller* with cheese, and coffee, and then more coffee, and in the afternoon a beer.

Magnus comes on the train from Copenhagen on Friday night with his laundry. Lene and Jesper come from Odense with Freja and Oliver, who has grown a moustache since the funeral and is clearly waiting for someone to mention it, and nobody does. Bent rings at nine to sing *I dag er det* down the phone, badly, all the verses, and to say his hip is bad and he can't drive, and *happy birthday, knægt*, and something else that you don't catch because Freja is screaming at Ida about something on her phone in the next room.

It's a good day. It's a perfectly good day. That's the strange thing. You sit at the head of the table with a paper flag sticking out of your slice of cake, and you open presents: a book about the history of Danish land surveying from Magnus, who has clearly asked his mother; a bottle of whisky from Jesper; a jumper from Lene; a framed print from Ida, which turns out to be the scan of Harold's photograph of the white farmhouse in Elk Horn, *Harold og Dorothy, 1962*, cleaned up and enlarged, the girl on the porch with her hand up against the sun.

"I did it at school," Ida says. "In the media lab. I took out the scratches."

You look at it for a long time. Dorothy on the steps, twenty-one, about to be a teacher, never having seen the sea.

---
