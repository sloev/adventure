:: c5-week
Easter comes in the second week of April, wet and windy and late-feeling, the way Easter does when it's early in the year. Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, Easter Sunday, Easter Monday: four days off in a row, which in Denmark means everybody goes to their summer house or their parents', and the roads to the west coast are full on Wednesday afternoon, and Aarhus is half empty, and the supermarkets sell out of eggs.

You don't go anywhere. Mette has to work the Saturday, her second week as ward sister, because Annette left her a rota with a hole in it, and she comes home that evening so tired that she falls asleep on the sofa in her coat. Magnus comes home from Copenhagen for the long weekend, with a bag of washing and a girlfriend you didn't know about, called Asta, who is studying chemistry and calls you *Søren* very confidently on the doorstep and eats three helpings of the Easter lamb. Ida likes her immediately, which surprises everyone, including Ida.

On Easter Sunday, at lunch, there is *påskefrokost*: herring three ways, eggs with prawns, the lamb, the cheese board, snaps from the freezer in little glasses that frost over in your hand. The yellow paper daffodils on the table. The children too old now for the egg hunt in the garden and doing it anyway, Ida and Magnus and Asta, in the rain, with Mette shouting *there's one in the gutter* from the kitchen door.

It's a good Easter. You notice yourself noticing that, the way you noticed it on your birthday. As if a good day had become something you check for now, like the post.

*Hi Søren. Thanks for your message, and sorry it took me so long to answer. I've written this answer many times. I think I know why we match. You're not the first who has written to me, and you won't be the last. I'm sorry to hear about your father. Can we talk properly? Not on the phone, I think. You read it in the office with the door closed, and then you read it again, and then you go downstairs, where Mette is asleep on the sofa in front of a film with her new ward-sister lanyard still round her neck, and you don't wake her. *There are several of us.*

She knows. Whatever it is, she knows it, and she's been carrying it long enough to write *it's a lot to be told* with the gentleness of someone who was once told it herself.

In the summer, she said. Not yet. *When it gets a bit lighter.*

You find you're grateful to her for that. You hadn't known you needed someone to tell you to wait.

:: c5-viborg
You leave Viby at a quarter past seven on the Tuesday, with a Thermos of coffee and two rye-bread sandwiches with *rullepølse* that Mette made you at six while you were in the shower, without being asked, and wrapped in greaseproof paper, the way her mother did for her father's fishing trips. In the bag on the passenger seat: the notebook, three pencils, a pencil sharpener, your phone, a charger, a small tripod for the phone that you bought at Elgiganten on Saturday and practised with on the kitchen table, photographing a page of the Jyllands-Posten, until Ida took it away from you and showed you the settings.

The motorway north-west. Silkeborg, where Camilla Bruun lives, somewhere among those lakes and pine trees, off to the left. The long straight road over the heath past Kjellerup. Fields brown and green and the first rape coming yellow at the edges. A buzzard on a fence post, looking at nothing. The sky enormous and grey and moving fast from the west.

Viborg sits on its hill above the lakes with the twin towers of the cathedral on top of it like a pair of ears. You've been here for work, twice, to the land registry court. You've never been here for this.

The archive is not where the brown sign by the motorway suggested in January. It's in town, a pale modern building on a quiet street below the cathedral, with a car park, a bicycle rack, a flagpole, and glass doors that open by themselves. Inside it's warm and very quiet and smells of floor polish and paper. A reception desk. A rack of leaflets. A woman of about sixty behind the desk in a grey cardigan with reading glasses on a chain, who looks up as you come in, and you know before she says anything that she is the kind of person who has spent thirty years being kind to frightened people with cardboard boxes.

"Good morning. Have you booked?"

"Søren Kjær. Seat eleven."

She checks a screen. "Kjær. Holstebro Ret, retsopgøret, package seventeen." She looks at you over her glasses. "You've been here before?"

"Never."

"Then I'll show you." She comes round the desk. Her name badge says *Anne-Marie Dahl, Læsesalen*. "Lockers here. Everything goes in except paper, pencils, your phone or camera and a laptop if you've got one. No pens. No bags. No food or drink, not even water. No coats. Sigtede er overført til Interneringslejren i Holstebro og derfra under 11. Maj 1945 til Fårhuslejren.

*Arrested on 8 May 1945, at about two o'clock, at his place of work, Struer Trælast, by members of the resistance movement, and handed over to the police.* The timber yard by the harbour in Struer. You know it; it's a builders' merchant now, with a car park where the timber stacks were. Three days after the liberation. Members of the resistance, with armbands, probably, and rifles, probably, and some of them, probably, boys he'd been at school with.

*Fårhuslejren.* You look it up on your phone, there and then, holding the phone low under the edge of the table as though it were a crime: the old German prison camp at Frøslev, near the border, which after the liberation was renamed and filled with Danes the Germans had left behind them. Up to five thousand at a time. Aksel was one of them.

You photograph the page. *Click.* Turn it from the corner. *Click.*

Then the statement.

It's six pages, handwritten by a police officer in a fast, clear, slanting hand, in the first person, as if Aksel were speaking, the way Danish police statements were written: *Afh. forklarer…* the person interviewed explains. Signed at the bottom of each page by the officer, and at the end, under the words *Oplæst og vedtaget*, read aloud and accepted, by Aksel himself.

You've never seen his signature. It's a young man's signature: careful, rounded, a schoolboy's *A* with a little loop, and *Kjær* with the *æ* joined up, the way they taught it in the village school in Holstebro Landsogn in 1928.

You read the statement once through without photographing it. You can't help it. It's in ordinary handwriting, not Gothic; it's in ordinary Danish, only a little old-fashioned. It takes you twenty-five minutes. You don't hear the man with the tripod, or the couple murmuring, or anything at all.

You sit back.

*The questioned man explains that in the spring of 1941 he was unemployed and had been for over half a year. His father had been ill with his lungs since 1938 and could not work, and there were two younger siblings. He had looked for work in Struer and Holstebro and in Germany, but got nothing.*

*He had in 1940 for some months been a member of the NSU in Holstebro, mostly for the sake of the football, and there he heard about the Frikorps. He volunteered in July 1941 at the office in Copenhagen. He did it because he had no work and because he believed that Bolshevism was the greatest danger to Denmark and to Finland, and because the government itself had said it was permitted. He has never been a member of the DNSAP.*

*Training at Treskau near Posen until spring 1942, then with the corps to the front at Demyansk in May 1942. He took part in the fighting at Demyansk and on 9 June 1942 was wounded in the left shoulder by shell splinters. Hospital in Riga and later in Germany; sent home in February 1943 and discharged from the corps. Since then not in German service; worked at Struer Trælast.*

*The questioned man would like to add that he regrets volunteering. He thought he was going to fight for Finland. That was not how it was. He does not wish to say more about the front.*

Not an email: a letter, a real one, in a thick cream envelope with a window, franked in Copenhagen, addressed to *Søren Poul Kjær* in full, which nobody ever writes. The logo in the corner is two overlapping circles and the words *Nordisk Arveudredning ApS*.

You stand in the hall with it, under the cupboard with the coats.

Nordic Heir Research.
