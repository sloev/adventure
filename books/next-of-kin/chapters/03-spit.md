:: c3-spit
You do it at a quarter to ten, at the kitchen table, under the lamp, with the instructions unfolded in front of you, because you always read the instructions.

*Do not eat, drink, smoke or chew gum for 30 minutes before collecting your sample.* You haven't had anything since the tea at nine. You wait until a quarter past, watching the kitchen clock.

The kit is very small for what it is. A plastic tube with a funnel on top, like something from a child's chemistry set. A cap with a blue liquid sealed inside it. A plastic bag with a strip of adhesive. A prepaid envelope addressed to a laboratory in Texas, via a returns depot in Germany. A card with a long activation code. That's all. That's the whole thing that is supposed to tell you where you come from.

You register the kit on the website first, as instructed, typing in the code with two fingers, choosing *yourself* from the dropdown that asks *whose DNA is this?*, ticking boxes that say you consent to having your DNA processed and compared to other users, unticking one that says you consent to having it used for research, then ticking it again, because you can't think of a reason not to, then unticking it again, because you can't think of a reason to.

Then you spit.

It takes longer than you'd think. The tube wants about two millilitres, up to a black line, not counting bubbles, and your mouth, which has produced saliva without being asked for forty-nine years, suddenly won't. You sit at the kitchen table spitting into a funnel like a man with a very specific grievance. Ida comes down for a glass of water at one point, looks at you, and goes back upstairs without a word, shaking with silent laughter.

When it's up to the line you screw on the cap, which releases the blue liquid into the tube with a little click, and you shake it for five seconds as instructed, and it turns pale blue, and that's it. That's you. Two millilitres of blue.

You put it in the bag and the bag in the envelope, and the envelope on the hall table by your keys, and in the morning, on the way to the car, you post it in the red postbox outside the Netto on Viby Torv, and you stand there for a moment with your hand on the cold metal of the slot, the way you stood by the container in Struer.

Then you go to work.

:: c3-file
On Wednesday the fourth you look for Aksel.

Not the church books. You know where he was born and who his parents were. You look for the other thing: the case. *Landssvigersag.* You've been reading about it in the evenings, in the background of everything else, the way you'd read up on a planning law before a hearing: the *retsopgør*, the legal reckoning after the war, when about forty thousand Danes were arrested in the weeks after liberation and some thirteen thousand were eventually convicted, under laws passed in 1945 that applied backwards to things done in 1940. Most of the convicted were people who had served in German uniform. Frikorps Danmark. Waffen-SS. The Schalburg Corps. The HIPO. Most of them got between one and four years. A few, for murder and torture, were executed.

The case files, the court papers, the police interviews, are in Rigsarkivet. You find, after an hour of clicking through the archives' online catalogue, a system called Daisy that looks as if it was designed in 2004 by a committee of librarians and has not been touched since, the series that should contain it: the court records of the retsopgør for the jurisdiction of Holstebro, transferred from the courts to the archive in Viborg, *Straffesager, 1945–1949*, with a box list. The box list is by case number, not by name. There's a separate name register, scanned, handwritten, alphabetical. You go through it image by image, *K*, *Kj*, and there it is, in a clerk's tidy blue hand:

```doc Rigsarkivet · Daisy · Holstebro Ret, Retsopgøret, Register til straffesager 1945–1949, opslag 54
Kjær, Aksel, Arbejdsmand, Struer. f. 12.5.21. Frikorps Danmark. Sag nr. 1946/112. Pk. 17.
```

*Frikorps Danmark.* It's just there. In the register, in blue ink, in the space where the clerk wrote what each man had done, the way you'd write *hedge* or *ditch* on a boundary survey. Not hidden. Not even unusual. Above Aksel there's a *Kjeldsen, Henning, Landmand, Ulfborg: Schalburgkorpset*. Below him a *Kjærgaard, Viggo, Chauffør, Holstebro: Værnemagerarbejde*: worked for the Germans. Three men in a row. A page of them.

Package 17. Case 1946/112.

You click the package. A grey box comes up: *This archival unit contains information that is not freely accessible. To see it, you must apply for access. Expected processing time: 2–6 weeks. If access is granted, the material will be made available in the reading room you select.*

There is a form. *Ansøgning om adgang til arkivalier.* It asks for your name, your address, your CPR number, the reference of the material, and a field called *Formål*, purpose, with a box for free text, and a dropdown of standard purposes, of which one is *Slægtsforskning*, genealogy, and another is *Videnskabelig forskning*, scholarly research, and another is *Andet*, other.

You sit with the cursor in the purpose box for a long time.

If you send it, in two to six weeks you'll be able to drive two hours to Viborg and sit in a reading room and open a cardboard box, and inside it will be your grandfather's own statement, in his own words, about why he put on that uniform. And a police officer's notes. And a judge's sentence. And whatever else a Danish court in 1946 thought worth writing down about a twenty-four-year-old warehouseman from Struer.

You think about Bent on the landing at the Havnehotel. *What he did when he was twenty, he paid for. That's it. That's all there is.*

:: c3-emigrants
On Friday night you go to America.

The Danish Emigration Archives, *Det Danske Udvandrerarkiv*, are in Aalborg, in an old warehouse on the harbour, and their database is online: three hundred and ninety thousand Danes who left the country through Copenhagen between 1869 and 1908, typed up from the Copenhagen police's emigration protocols, because emigration agents had to register every ticket they sold with the police. You type *Overgaard* and *Hansted*, and the results come back in a list, and you know him at once.

```doc Det Danske Udvandrerarkiv · Emigrantdatabasen · Københavns Politis udvandrerprotokoller
| Navn | Erhverv | Alder | Sidste bopæl | Bestemmelsessted | Kontrakt nr. | Dato | Skib |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overgaard, Jens Peter | Fisker | 21 | Hansted, Thisted amt | Elk Horn, Iowa | 117/843 | 18.4.1903 | Oscar II |
| Overgaard, Søren Peder | Fisker | 23 | Hansted, Thisted amt | Buenos Aires | 32/2210 | 9.10.1906 | (via Hamburg) |
```

Jens Peter. Twenty-one. Fisherman. Elk Horn, Iowa, eighteenth of April 1903, on the *Oscar II*, the new ship of the Scandinavian America Line, which a quick search shows you in a photograph: black hull, two masts and one red-and-black funnel, at the quay in Copenhagen with a crowd on the dock in long coats and hats. He'd have gone by train from Thisted to Copenhagen, the whole length of the country, his first time out of Thy, with a wooden trunk. Then eleven days across the Atlantic to New York. Then the train again: Chicago, Omaha, a branch line, a cart. To a place where his cousins or neighbours had gone before him, because nobody went to Elk Horn by chance.

And Søren Peder, three years later, twenty-three, fisherman, to *Buenos Aires*. Not America at all, or not the America anyone in Thy meant when they said it. *Amerika*, the family would have said. *He's gone to Amerika.* And he went to Argentina, and came back, and played the guitar.

You find Jens Peter in the American records on FamilySearch, which is free, and which the Mormons have built, Ida says, *because they baptise dead people, far, it's a whole thing, don't worry about it*. 1910 United States Federal Census, Iowa, Shelby County, Clay Township: *Overgaard, Jens P., head, 28, born Denmark, immigrated 1903, farmer, rents farm. Anna, wife, 24, born Iowa, parents born Denmark. Harold, son, 0 years, born Iowa.*

Harold, aged zero. Harold, who in 1962 would write *Kære Fætter Niels* in loopy blue ballpoint and buy Nielsen's land.

1920: they own the farm now. 1930: there's a daughter, Ruth. 1940: Jens P. is fifty-eight; Harold is thirty and married to a woman called Evelyn, and there's no Dorothy yet; she's born the next year. 1950: Dorothy, nine, *at school*.

You type *Dorothy Overgaard Elk Horn* into Google.

You get a page of results. A 1959 high-school yearbook from Elk Horn–Kimballton, scanned by a local-history group, with a photograph of a girl with a ponytail and cat's-eye glasses: *Dorothy Overgaard — "Dot" — FHA, Choir, Library Club. "Still waters run deep."* A list of teachers at Harlan Community School, 1962–1999. And a newsletter from the Museum of Danish America, in Elk Horn, from the autumn of 2017, a PDF, with a photograph of a small, white-haired woman in a cardigan, standing beside a large wooden chest with iron bands and a painted lid:

*Our thanks to Dorothy "Dot" Overgaard of Elk Horn, retired Harlan schoolteacher, for the gift of her grandfather Jens Peter Overgaard's emigrant trunk, which came over with him on the Oscar II in 1903. The trunk still bears its original painted address: "J.P. Overgaard, Elk Horn, Iowa, Amerika." Dot tells us she has "always meant to see the North Sea."*

You sit back.

She's seventy-six in that photograph. She'd be eighty-four or eighty-five now. She never married; the newsletter says *Miss Overgaard*. She has always meant to see the North Sea. *She has never seen the sea,* Harold wrote, in 1962. *I tell her it is colder than she thinks.*

She might be alive. She might be sitting in a farmhouse in Iowa tonight, at eighty-four, in a cardigan, with Harold's letters from Thy in a box somewhere. Karen's second cousin. *She was going to come. She wrote to me. She sent me a picture of herself in a cap and gown.*

At the bottom of the newsletter: *The Genealogy Center is staffed by volunteers Tuesday–Saturday. Questions? genealogy@* and an address.

On Sunday the fifteenth, Fastelavn, you drive to Struer alone, and Fjordglimt is hitting the cat out of the barrel.
