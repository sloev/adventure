# Next of Kin: what we take from a real family-history project

The book is inspired by a real, public Danish genealogy project: [github.com/sloev/tree](https://github.com/sloev/tree) (*Slægten Gårdsted og Gedde*, its research claims in `content/claims/`, method in `research_method.md`). It shows what real research in Danish records actually looks like in 2026: the sources, the dead ends, the family legends, the errors on MyHeritage, the court record that says one thing and the family another, and a researcher working with AI agents and a strict evidence standard.

**Rule: borrow patterns, never people.** No name, place-and-date combination, photo or story of a real person from that tree goes into the book. Characters, farms and dates in *Next of Kin* are invented; what we copy is the *shape* of real findings and the *texture* of real work. Living people and DNA data from that project are off-limits entirely, even as inspiration for specifics.

## Patterns to use

| Pattern seen in real research | How it appears in *Next of Kin* |
|---|---|
| **A grandparent's "secret father"**: a child born months after a hurried marriage; a court registers one man as father after a declaration; the family names another; the man paid to marry. The son learns at 40. A court finding is not genetic proof; DNA from the right cousin line could settle it. | The 1898 Overgaard paternity case (canon §5, minor finds): unsolvable by DNA five generations back, and the reader learns to say so. Thematic echo of Søren's own donor story. |
| **Family legend vs proof**: a family "descends" from a famous admiral with a near-identical surname; the real ancestor is a different, humbler officer family. The research keeps the legend visible, labelled as legend. | The Thomsen legend of "Kaptajn Thomsen" and the Danish West Indies (canon §6). |
| **The dazzling deep line**: a branch that seems to run to medieval nobility through printed genealogies, where each link must be checked and the earliest links can't be. | The AI helper / an online tree offers Søren a line to 13th-century nobility; the reader can accept it or audit it (canon §6). |
| **MyHeritage tree errors**: a wrong death year copied into hundreds of trees (1904 vs 1907 in the burial register); a family tree that wrongly moves a line to Bornholm when censuses show it never left the parish. | Søren's first wrong merge (S2); Birthe's correction; the habit of checking every "Smart Match" against the church book. |
| **Patronymics and identical names**: two possible mothers called Marthe for a child born out of wedlock and raised as a foster child; neither is proven. | An *uægte* child in 1798 with two candidate fathers (canon §5). Some questions stay open at the end of the book. |
| **Emigration that isn't simple**: parents emigrate with seven of nine children and *come back*; "Amerika" in a family story may mean Argentina; one son to Argentina, one to Shanghai, one washed overboard from a training ship. | Overgaard siblings: Jens Peter to Iowa (the estate), a brother who went to Argentina and returned, a boy lost at sea from a training ship (minor finds). |
| **Local legend figure**: a smallholder who bought an islet and built a causeway to it by hand, called "the King of" the island in a granddaughter's memoir held at the local archive. | An Overgaard who leased a holm in the fjord and "King of" nickname, known only from a lokalarkiv memoir (minor finds; Kurt has the memoir). |
| **Death clusters**: three sons dead within two years around 1910 (TB era). | One Thomsen household, 1911–12 (minor finds), found in the burial register; nobody alive remembers. |
| **Borderland records**: Sønderjylland 1864–1920 under Prussian rule: German civil registers (*Standesamt*), German names, the Aabenraa reading room. | Hans-Jørgen's adoptive family in Sønderborg (S9); the adoption file route via Aabenraa. |
| **A strict evidence standard**: every claim has *Claim / Evidence / Missing / Next*; validation from *lead* to *confirmed*; record what a source does *not* prove; record negative searches; OCR is never authoritative, every handwritten image is read by eye; legal, biological, foster and household relations are never merged. | Søren's research binder becomes the reader's tool. A new `claim` block shows his working notes; the book rewards readers who keep claims honest (see bible, "The binder"). |
| **Public vs private**: living people and people born after ~1926 without a death date are hidden in the published tree; DNA match data is never published. | Chapter 12 (*The tree*): what Søren may, can and should publish, and to whom. |
| **AI agents doing research**: an AI-assisted intake pipeline (OCR, transcription drafts, claims), with humans checking every page because the model misreads Gothic script and over-connects identities. | Optional thread: Ida sets Søren up with an AI assistant (`AIHELPER`). It is fast, helpful and wrong in specific, realistic ways (S2, S5, S12). |
| **Real sources and finding aids**: Arkivalieronline, Link-Lives, Danish Family Search, DAISY archive catalogue and ordering, Slægtsforskernes Bibliotek PDFs, arkiv.dk, *Dansk Jord* farm indexes, county yearbooks (*årbøger*), midwife protocols, probate (*skifte*) records, *standsregistre*. | Props throughout. Show the actual catalogue routes (series, volume, package number), as a real researcher would note them. |
