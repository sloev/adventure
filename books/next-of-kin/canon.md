## 1. The photograph: Aksel Kjær and Frikorps Danmark

- **Aksel Kjær**, b. 12 May 1921 in Holstebro landsogn, son of a farm labourer. Unemployed in 1941; briefly in the DNSAP youth organisation (NSU) in Holstebro, mostly for the football and the uniform.
- **Volunteered for Frikorps Danmark in July 1941**, aged 20. Trained in Germany; served on the Eastern Front; **wounded at Demyansk in June 1942** (shell splinters, left shoulder). Discharged, back in Denmark in early 1943. Worked as a warehouseman in Struer. Not in the Schalburg Corps; no further German service.
- **Arrested 8 May 1945** at Struer Trælast by resistance men; Holstebro internment camp, then **Fårhuslejren** (the former Frøslev camp) from 11 May 1945. His father Niels Peter had been ill with his lungs since 1938 and could not work. Training at **Treskau**; Demyansk from May 1942; wounded **9 June 1942**; hospital in Riga and Germany; discharged Feb 1943. Statement of 2 Oct 1945: regrets it, "Det var ikke saadan det var". **Margin note (pencil): admits to having *witnessed* the shooting of partisans at Demyansk, denies participation.** Judgment 4 April 1946, Holstebro Ret, § 10 (2 years); parole 8 Sept 1946, prisoner no. 2214. Sentenced in **1946** under the retroactive treason legislation (*straffelovstillægget*) to **2 years' imprisonment** (frontline service, no aggravating circumstances). Internment from May 1945 counted towards the sentence; **released on parole (*prøveløsladelse*) in September 1946** after two-thirds. (Bent remembers only "two years".)
- His **case file** (*landssvigersag*) is in Rigsarkivet: police interview, his own statement ("I wanted to fight Bolshevism and I had no work"), a character reference from a former employer, the court record. Accessible in 2026 in the Viborg reading room after an online application; delivery about three weeks. It includes a **photograph** of him in uniform: the same one as the shoebox photo, where someone (Inger) later scratched out the collar patch.
- The family story, as Bent tells it: "Far was in Germany in the war. He went to fight the Russians, like the Finns did. They put him in a camp for it afterwards. He never talked about it." True in each sentence and false as a whole.
- Aksel married Inger on **15 February 1947** at Struer Kirke (Poul born 21 August 1947). He was a quiet, strict, funny father; good with engines; never voted; drank on 4 May every year and nobody said why. Died of a heart attack in 1989, when Søren was 12. Søren remembers him fondly.
- **Not Søren's blood** (see §3). Søren carries his name, his laugh (learned), and his toolbox (via Poul).

## 2. The other child: Inger Bach and Hans-Jørgen

- **Inger Bach**, b. 1923, daughter of a Struer harbour worker. In 1941, aged 18, she had a relationship with a German soldier, **Gefreiter Walter Renz** (b. 1919, Esslingen near Stuttgart), stationed at Struer in 1941. He was transferred in autumn 1941 and **killed in Italy in 1944**.
- Inger gave birth on **17 March 1942** at a maternity home for unmarried mothers in Aarhus. The boy was adopted that summer by a childless couple in Sønderborg, **the Nielsens**, and named **Hans-Jørgen Nielsen**. A receipt/notification of the adoption (*adoptionsbevilling* correspondence) is folded into Inger's hymn book in the shoebox.
- The caseworker's file (part of the welfare/adoption records in Rigsarkivet, restricted; access requires a dispensation application, which a descendant of Inger's *paper* family has standing to request only weakly; Hans-Jørgen himself would have full standing) contains a note that "the girl is of weak character" and a suggestion that **Sprogø** be considered if there were "a further incident". She was **not** sent to Sprogø. Writers: this is where the women's institution and the sterilisation laws may enter, through a caseworker's sentence, not through melodrama.
- In **May 1945** Inger was harassed as a *tyskerpige* in Struer (a crowd; her hair was not cut but it was threatened). She met Aksel in the autumn of 1946, soon after his release, through her brother. Two people the town had an opinion about. They never told their sons about either thing.
- **Karen knew** that Inger "had a child before, in the war". Inger told her in 1993, at Poul's kitchen table, after a funeral. Karen promised not to tell Poul. She didn't.
- **Hans-Jørgen Nielsen**, b. 1942, alive in 2026 in Sønderborg: retired electrician, widower, two daughters. He has known since 1968 that he was adopted and since 2015 who his birth mother was (he got his file). He wrote to Inger in 2009; she did not answer. He never knew she had other children. His younger daughter **Susanne** has tested on MyHeritage; she appears in **Bent's** match list at about **880 cM** (MyHeritage: "1st cousin – half uncle/niece"; Bent is her half-uncle) but never in Søren's.
- Hans-Jørgen is Poul's and Bent's **half-brother**. Not Søren's blood either.

## 3. The match list: Søren is donor-conceived

- **Poul Kjær was infertile** after mumps orchitis at 22 (1969). He and Karen went to a private gynaecologist in Holstebro, **Dr Erik Lund-Madsen** (1931–2014), who offered "insemination with an anonymous donor, a medical student from Aarhus". There was no medical student. **The donor was Lund-Madsen himself.** He did this for at least two decades.
- **Søren (b. 1977) and Lene (b. 1980) are full siblings**, both conceived this way. Their DNA match each other normally (~2,600 cM), which is why nothing ever looked odd between them.
- **Karen and Poul knew it was a donor.** They did not know it was the doctor. They agreed never to tell anyone, including the children. Poul's DNA kit sat unopened because he knew that Ida's and Søren's results would show no link to him and he could not decide what to do about it. He meant to decide after New Year.
- In 2026 at least **23 half-siblings** have found each other through DNA (born 1968–1991, mostly Holstebro, Struer, Lemvig). They identified the doctor in 2023 through his grandnephew's test and an obituary. They have a Messenger group. **Camilla Bruun** (b. 1979, Silkeborg) is the group's de facto archivist and the first to appear in Søren's/Ida's list (Søren: ~1,750 cM, "close family, half sibling or uncle/nephew"; Ida: ~900 cM).
- The doctor's legitimate son, **Peter Lund-Madsen**, 66, retired lawyer in Aarhus, has refused to test and has asked the group, through a lawyer's letter, not to contact him again.
- **What the match list shows** (both Søren's and Ida's): Overgaard/Thomsen cousins from Thy as expected; **no Kjær or Bach matches at all** where second and third cousins are statistically certain to exist (Bent's grandchildren have tested on MyHeritage: they would be expected at second-cousin-once-removed range); and a cluster of strangers from Holstebro at half-sibling and half-niece level.
- **Ida tested in January regardless of the reader's choices.** Her results arrive on **Saturday 28 February** (end of ch3); the full match list is read in ch4. The match-list thread therefore reaches Søren whether or not he tests, though if he doesn't test he can choose to believe for longer that it's "an error".
- **Lene** does not want to know. She is not wrong to feel that.

## 4. The letter from Iowa: the Overgaard estate

- **Jens Peter Overgaard** (1881–1957), Karen's great-uncle, emigrated in **1903** via Copenhagen (on the Copenhagen police emigration protocols; findable in the Udvandrerarkiv database) to **Elk Horn, Shelby County, Iowa**, where he farmed. His son **Harold** (1910–1988) wrote letters to Christen's family until the 1970s; the **1962 airmail letter** in the shoebox is Harold to Niels (Karen's father), with a photo of the farm and of his daughter Dorothy, then 21.
- **Dorothy "Dot" Overgaard** (1941–2026), retired schoolteacher, never married, no children, siblings or first cousins; lived on the home place near Elk Horn and kept the **240 acres** of farmland, leased to a neighbour. **She died on 3 February 2026 without a will.** Estate around **US$2.9 million** (mostly land), opened in the Shelby County district court; administrator appointed by the court.
- Her heirs at law are the descendants of her **great-grandparents Peder Jensen Overgaard and Maren Christensdatter** (writers: confirm how far Iowa intestate succession reaches and how shares are divided, per `FACTCHECK.md`, before writing the numbers into prose). Two Danish branches have living descendants: **Christen's** (Karen and her cousins) and **Ane Marie's** (the Vestergaards, **including Holger, Mette's father**).
- **Nordisk Arveudredning ApS** (Copenhagen, invented), working for a US heir-search partner, finds the Danish heirs from Danish records within weeks and writes to each in **May 2026**, offering to reveal the estate in return for **33 %** of any share. Legal, standard practice. Holger signs within a day.
- The estate is findable without the firm: the 1962 letter names Elk Horn and Dorothy; Find a Grave has her memorial (posted February 2026); the obituary in the local paper mentions "cousins in Denmark"; the court case is listed on the state's public court portal. A reader who takes the `DIY` route can contact the administrator's attorney directly.
- **Karen has dementia** and cannot sign anything. To act for her, Søren needs to be appointed her guardian (*værge*) through **Familieretshuset** (or use a *fremtidsfuldmagt*, which Karen never made). Karen **dies on Sunday 22 November 2026**; her share then passes to her estate, which Søren and Lene inherit. Distribution from the Iowa estate happens after the book ends (or is pending in the epilogue).
- Karen's share, whatever the law makes it, is real money but not a fortune after the firm's cut (if signed), attorneys' fees and the time it takes. Writers: keep numbers honest and modest.

## 5. The dunes: the Overgaard deep line, and Mette

- The Overgaards are in Vigsø/Hansted parish records from the **1680s**: a **Jens Overgaard** listed as a tenant (*fæster*) in the 1688 land register (Christian V's matrikel), on land that was later lost to **sand drift** in the 1790s; the family moved a kilometre inland. Fishermen, smallholders, a parish clerk in the 1760s, a beach bailiff (*strandfoged*) in the 1830s, two men drowned in a storm in 1862.
- **Minor finds anywhere on this line** (writers may use any, in any chapter): a boy, Christen, recorded as *uægte* (illegitimate) in 1798, born to Ane Christensdatter before she married Peder Jensen (Søren's ancestor) in 1799; the father column has one name crossed out and another written above it, neither of them Peder; Christen grew up in Peder's house ("Hustruens uægte Søn (?)" in the 1801 census); a half-sibling of Søren's direct line, **never resolved**; a twin who died the same day; a theft-of-wreckage fine in 1821; a farm called *Overgaard* that is now a holiday park; a confirmation record where the priest wrote "knows his catechism poorly"; a soldier in the 1864 war who came home; a brother of Jens Peter, **Søren Peder Overgaard** (1883–1968), who went to "Amerika" in 1906, which turns out to be Argentina (the Danish colony near Tandil), and came back in 1912 with nothing and a guitar; a boy of 15 lost overboard from a training ship in 1899; three Thomsen sons dead of tuberculosis in 1911–12; an Overgaard who leased a holm (islet) in the Limfjord and built a stone causeway to it by hand, called "*Kongen af Holmen*" in a granddaughter's memoir held by the Hurup lokalarkiv.
- **The 1898 paternity case.** Christen Overgaard's younger cousin Marie (b. 1877) married a farmhand in March 1898; her son was born in May. A paternity declaration in the court record names the farmhand; a family story told by Kurt's mother names the farmer's son where Marie was in service. DNA cannot settle it at this distance with the testers available. It stays open. (This is a side line: not Søren's direct ancestry, and it does not affect any cM figure above.)

## 6. The legend: "Kaptajn Thomsen"

- Karen's mother's family (Thomsen, Hanstholm) has a story that they descend from **"Kaptajn Thomsen"**, a sea captain who sailed for the Asiatic Company to Tranquebar and the Danish West Indies in the 1780s, and that "there's a portrait in a museum".
- **Truth:** there was a Captain Hans Thomsen in the Company's service (invented person), from Copenhagen, unrelated. Søren's actual ancestor of that name is **Thomas Hansen Thomsen** (1771–1839), a **ship's carpenter** in Hanstholm, who did go to sea once, in 1791, as carpenter on a merchant ship to Norway. The legend grew from a name and a voyage. Records: the *lægdsrulle* (levy roll) and a crew list; the captain's own family is documented elsewhere.
- **The noble line.** An online tree (and, if `AIHELPER`, the AI assistant, confidently) attaches the captain to a German noble family and from there to the 1200s. Every link after the captain is someone else's research copied without sources; the captain link itself is wrong. A reader who audits it finds the break at the first step. A reader who doesn't publishes it in Ch12, and a stranger in the DNA group politely corrects him in public.
- The legend is harmless and beloved. Mette's father Holger tells it too (it's his family as well, via Kirstine Thomsen), with a different captain.
- **Søren and Mette are double third cousins.** They share two couples of great-great-grandparents: **Peder Jensen Overgaard & Maren Christensdatter** (Mette through Ane Marie Overgaard → Anders Vestergaard → Holger), and **Thøger Thomsen & Johanne Kirstine Sørensdatter** of Hanstholm (Søren through his grandmother Edel Thomsen; Mette through her grandmother Kirstine Thomsen, Edel's first cousin). DNA: ~150 cM shared. Medically insignificant. Socially: a joke in Thy, a shock in Viby, and the reason both Søren and Holger got heir letters.
- Neither family knew. Holger "always thought there was something" about Karen's family and won't say what he meant.
- This thread finds nothing terrible. It is where the reader can be happy.
