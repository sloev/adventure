:: s6-open
June arrives all at once, the way it does in Berlin: one week you're wearing a jacket on the bike, and the next the city is thirty degrees and smells of linden blossom and hot tarmac, and everyone you know is sitting on the canal bank with their feet in the water and a bottle of Sternburg, and the swans have cygnets.

The fourth floor gets hot. The old factory was built in the 1890s to keep machines cool in summer and warm in winter, and it does neither. The arched windows face south-west. By three in the afternoon the long room is twenty-nine degrees, and Facilities has distributed twelve desk fans, one per row, which push the hot air from one end of the room to the other and back. There's no air conditioning. There has never been air conditioning. Every June someone in `#random` posts a photo of a thermometer, and every June Facilities replies with a link to a Confluence page titled *Staying Cool at Kontor* that recommends drinking water and wearing light clothing.

This June nobody posts the photo. People are quieter in June. Fifteen people where there were nineteen. Desks that nobody sits at. The fruit delivery stopped on the first of June — a two-line email from Facilities, *as part of our cost-efficiency measures*, and the big wooden bowl in the kitchen where the apples and bananas used to be now holds a single dried-up lime that nobody will throw away, because throwing it away would mean admitting something.

On Tuesday the second, at 17:00, there's the works meeting.

:: s6-meeting
The Kantine at five o'clock on Tuesday the second of June is hotter than the fourth floor, and fuller than it's been since the twelfth of May.

The tables haven't been moved. People just sit at them, and on them, and stand along the walls, with bottles of water and fans made of folded agendas. Dorota has opened the big windows on the river side, and the noise of a tourist boat's loudspeaker drifts in — *on your left, the East Side Gallery, the longest surviving section of the Berlin Wall* — and makes some people laugh. There are maybe two hundred and twenty people. Out of three hundred and eighty-two left in the Berlin office. It's working time; Rebecca's email said so. Lars has walked over with most of Platform. Aylin with Data. Priya came down with Money Movement in a loose group and is sitting at the back, on the end of a table, as an employee like any other. There are no managers at the front. There's no stage.

There's a table at the end of the room by the salad bar, and behind it, three people. Fatma in the middle, with her folder. Sasha on her left, enormous, in a T-shirt that says *KHARKIV IS UKRAINE* in faded letters. And on her right, depending on what you did in a back room at Würgeengel three weeks ago, either you, sitting very straight and wishing you weren't, or Bettina from Support, cardigan and soothing voice and a glass of water, looking completely at ease.

At 17:03 Fatma stands up.

"*Guten Tag.* Good afternoon. I'm going to speak German and English, which makes everything twice as long, so please forgive me." Laughter. "My name is Fatma Yılmaz. I lead Customer Support. I've worked at Kontor for ten years. This is a works meeting under paragraph seventeen, section three of the Works Constitution Act, to elect an election board, a *Wahlvorstand*, of three people, who will organise the first ever election of a works council at Kontor Berlin." She looks round the room. "That's all this meeting is. It's not a protest. It's not against anybody. It's the law, and it's ours."

Someone at the back says "*Endlich*", and there's a ripple of applause, uncertain at first, then warmer.

"I want to introduce someone who will explain the rules better than I can," says Fatma. "She doesn't work here anymore. Technically she does until the thirtieth of June. She's on garden leave, so she's allowed to be here, and I asked her to come."

Simone stands up from a table near the front. Silver hair, shorthand notebook. You didn't see her come in.

She explains the rules. She does it in eight minutes, in a dry Leipzig voice, without notes, and it's the clearest explanation of anything you've heard all year. What a works council is and what it isn't. That it can't stop the company doing things, but it has to be consulted before them, and for some things — working hours, monitoring systems, the criteria for selecting people in a mass dismissal — it has to agree, or the company has to go to a conciliation board. That at Kontor's size, the council would have nine members. That anyone employed for six months can stand. That members can't be dismissed in the ordinary way while in office. That it costs the company money, time, and patience, and that the law requires the company to pay for it.

"I'll say one more thing," Simone says at the end. "In 2009 I worked at a bank in Leipzig that had a works council. When the bank was sold and they cut a third of us, the council negotiated for nine months. Nine months. Everyone hated it. Management hated it, we hated it, the newspapers called it obstruction." She looks at the room. "In the end, everyone who was cut got eighteen months' salary instead of six, and the criteria were written down and public, and everyone over fifty-five got a bridging pension. And the people who did the choosing had to put their names to it." She closes her notebook. "That's what slower buys you. That's all."

She sits down. The applause this time is not uncertain at all.

Then Fatma says: "Nominations for the election board. Three people. Anyone can nominate anyone, including themselves. Then we vote by hand." She picks up a pen. "Who would like to stand?"

There's the silence again. The retro silence. The *Stop pretending* silence, two hundred and twenty people deep, in the heat, with the tourist boat saying *on your right, the Oberbaum Bridge, built in 1896* through the open windows.

:: s6-stand
You stand up. Literally: you stand up from your chair, which you didn't plan to do, and you hear yourself say, "I'll stand."

Two hundred and twenty heads turn. You feel your face go hot, on top of the heat.

"Jakob Lenz," says Fatma, writing. "Money Movement." She looks up, and there's something in her face that you'll remember, not a smile exactly. "Thank you, Jakob. Would you like to say why?"

You didn't plan this either. You say the first true thing.

"Because I wasn't on the list on the twelfth," you say. "And I don't know why. And I don't know why the people who were, were. And I'd like there to be a next time where somebody knows."

There's a short silence. Then applause, real applause, and you sit down very fast, and Noor, beside you, puts her hand on your arm for half a second and takes it away.

Fatma takes two more nominations. Bettina, who stands and says, in her phone voice, "I've talked down ten thousand angry plumbers. I can talk to management." And Mats, the young Swede from Platform, who stands and says he's been here eighteen months and wants it written down that a junior engineer stood up too. And then, because there are only three places and three candidates, the vote is a show of hands for all three together, and two hundred and twenty hands go up, and a few don't, and Fatma counts them with Sasha anyway, slowly, row by row, because Simone says you have to, and writes the number down.

*Wahlvorstand: B. Kaminski (Vorsitz), J. Lenz, M. Lindgren.* Bettina as chair.

Afterwards, Rebecca sends you a Slack DM. One line.

Rebecca Lindqvist [18:41]: Congratulations. I meant what I said about finding it interesting to negotiate with you. Let me know what the election board needs from the company. R.

It's done in forty minutes. People drift out into the heat, talking, some of them laughing, a few of them looking as if they've done something slightly dangerous and enjoyed it. You put your hand up with everybody else. You were there. It's not nothing.

On the stairs, Sasha catches you up.

"Good meeting," he says.

He goes up the stairs two at a time, as he did in January in the bike cellar, leaving wet footprints. Not from rain, this time. From the heat.

:: s6-heat
With fifteen people instead of nineteen, the on-call rota for Money Movement's payment services has six names on it instead of eight, and two of those are Tech Lead Managers who are technically on it and practically never paged, by the same silent agreement that used to protect Priya. Which leaves four: you, Sasha, Tobi, and Noor, who was added in May after the twelfth, at her own request, because, she said, "if I'm the knowledge I have to be woken up by it."

Four people. A week each. You're on call every fourth week on paper, and every second week in practice, because when one of the four is on holiday or ill or has a child's summer party, the others cover. In June that's every second week. You start carrying the laptop everywhere. To the Kita. To the canal. To Tempelhofer Feld on a Sunday evening with Mila and a picnic, where you sit on a blanket on the old runway with the laptop in its sleeve beside the cheese and the strawberries, like a fourth member of the family.

The pages come at night, mostly. The heat makes everything worse: the banks' SFTP servers slow down, the PSP's latency creeps up in the afternoons, and at 02:20 on Thursday the eleventh the reconciler's ingest of the Berliner Sparkasse file fails because the file is truncated — the bank's own server, somewhere in a hot room in Berlin-Mitte, ran out of disk halfway through writing it. You sit at the kitchen table in your boxers with every window open and the courtyard full of the sound of other people's fans, and email the bank's technical hotline, which is staffed at night by a man called Herr Wolff who has been doing this since 1998 and who says, when he calls you back at 02:55, "*Ach, Kontor. Sie schon wieder.*" Oh, Kontor. You again. He regenerates the file. You re-run the ingest. You go back to bed at 03:40. Sanne doesn't wake up anymore when the submarine dives. That bothers you more than if she did.

At standup on the Friday, Otto's summary says: *Incidents: 1 (reconciler, SPK file truncation, resolved 03:38 by J. Lenz).* That's all. There's no line for *Herr Wolff* or *the kitchen table* or *Sanne didn't wake up*.

Burned, the agent you built in April — or watched Tobi and Noor build — is on every payment-path PR now. It flags what it can't verify. In June it flags forty-one things. Eleven of them matter. The review queue is down to thirty-something on a good day. Sasha says that's what victory looks like now: a slightly smaller tide.

Sasha writes the bracket on the kitchen whiteboard at 16:30 on Thursday the eighteenth. Sixteen names, not eight. At the top, in capitals: **LAST TOURNAMENT. NO SPINNING. EXCEPT TODAY.**

Noor has never played. Tobi has taught her in four days, on his lunch breaks, at this table, with a seriousness he used to bring to Otto. She plays defence. She doesn't spin. He passes the ball back to Noor's goalie. And waits.

:: s6-noor
On Tuesday the twenty-third, Noor asks you to come for a walk.

She never asks for walks. She asks for Krumme Lanke, or for ten minutes at the Brita tap, or she writes a careful DM that begins *Sorry to bother you*. Today she comes to your desk at 15:00 and says "Can we walk? Outside? Please," and you go.

You walk east along the river, past the Arena and the old harbour cranes and the floating pool, which is open now, full of shrieking teenagers, the blue water absurdly bright. It's thirty-one degrees. Noor walks fast, with her bag over her shoulder, and doesn't say anything until you're past the pool and onto the shady stretch under the plane trees by the Treptow bridge.

"I have an offer," she says.

You stop walking. She doesn't. You catch up.

"The Bundesbank," she says. "In Frankfurt. IT department. Payments systems. TARGET services." She almost smiles. "The real one. Not our calendar." She keeps walking. "A friend from the TU works there. She sent my CV in April. After the… after Simone told me about the spreadsheet. I did three interviews. In May. On my holidays. They called yesterday."

"Noor—"

"It's a *Beamten*-track position," she says, as if reading from a card. "Civil service, eventually. After probation, nobody can fire me. Ever. Unless I commit a crime." She laughs, a small surprised laugh, as if hearing it for the first time. "The salary is less. Eleven per cent less. Frankfurt is more expensive. My mother is in Berlin. My aunt sends me photos of the sea in Gaziantep every week and asks when I'm getting married." She stops walking, finally, under a plane tree, and turns to look at you. "And they don't use AI for anything. They have a committee that has been evaluating it since 2023. They still have a *fax machine*." She says *fax machine* the way you'd say *paradise*.

The river goes past, wide and slow and green in the shade.

"I have until Friday," she says. "And I don't know. I really don't know. I've been making lists for two days. Every list comes out even." She looks at you. "So I'm asking you. Not as a — not as a colleague. As someone who's seen it. What would you do?"

Wednesday the twenty-fourth of June is the last Schnitzel.

Nobody announces it. There's no email. There's just a sheet of A4 taped to the wall by the trays, under the weekly menu, in the same font the catering company has used since 2004:

```doc Kantine · Aushang
**Liebe Gäste,**

die Kantine schließt zum **26. Juni**. Wir bedanken uns für sechs schöne Jahre!

Ab 29. Juni steht Ihnen im EG ein **Smart Fridge** mit frischen Salaten, Bowls und Snacks zur Verfügung (Bezahlung per Mitarbeiterausweis).

Ihr Kantinen-Team
Dorota, Rüdiger, Ayşe, Maik
```

*Dear guests, the Kantine closes on 26 June. We thank you for six lovely years! From 29 June, a Smart Fridge with fresh salads, bowls and snacks will be available on the ground floor (payment by employee badge).* And then four names, which most of the people who've eaten here every day for six years have never seen written down. Dorota. Rüdiger. Ayşe, who does the salad bar. Maik, who washes up, and whom you've never once seen, because the dish room is behind the hatch.

By twelve the queue for Schnitzel goes out of the door and up the first flight of stairs. People who never eat in the Kantine are in it. People who left in May are in it: Felix, with his headphones round his neck; Hanne, with a new haircut; the karaoke man from Sales. They've come back for this. Ines sends a photo from Hamburg of a canteen Schnitzel, grey and deep-fried, with the caption *this is a crime*, and it's passed round the Settlements table on four phones.

Rüdiger makes three hundred and twelve Schnitzel. In a pan. Not the fryer. He doesn't come out from behind the hatch all day. You can see him through it, in his whites, at the big flat-top, beating the pork thin with a mallet, laying it in the breadcrumbs, turning it in the fat with his fingers, plating it, ringing the little bell. Three hundred and twelve times. His face never changes.

Dorota is at the till. She doesn't charge anybody. When people try to pay she waves the card reader away and says "*Heute nicht. Heute ist Abschied.*" Not today. Today's goodbye. When your turn comes, she looks at you over her red glasses for a long time.

"*Herr Fahrrad*," she says. "*Sie haben die Bremse immer noch nicht repariert.*" You still haven't fixed the brake. "*Ich höre Sie jeden Morgen. Sechs Jahre.*" I hear you every morning. Six years.
