:: s14-open
The alarm goes at 06:40, which is a lie you told yourself every Sunday night last year and still tell yourself now. You don't get up at 06:40. You get up at 06:52, when Mila climbs over Sanne and lands on your stomach with both knees and the full moral authority of a seven-year-old who has been awake since six and can now read.

"Papa. Papa. It's *Monday*."

"I know it's Monday."

"Axolotls," says Mila, "can regrow their *spinal cords*. I read it. Myself. In a book. At the Hort."

"Parts of their spinal cords," says Sanne, into her pillow.

"*All* of it," says Mila, who has read the book and knows, and goes off to find her socks, which will take eleven minutes and involve the whole flat.

It's still dark outside. It'll be dark until nearly eight. Through the gap in the curtain the streetlight on Herrfurthstraße shows a fine diagonal drizzle that can't commit to being snow. The radiator under the window ticks. It's the same radiator. It's the same drizzle. It's the same streetlight, the same gap in the curtain, the same orange stripe on the ceiling.

You lie there for one more minute doing the thing you've done every first-Monday-back for eight years, which is to run through your calendar in your head and feel the shape of the week settle on your chest like a cat.

It's a different shape this year.

:: s14-package-end
At ten you go down to the courtyard with a toolbox. The Hercules is locked to the railings, silent since July. You've been meaning to do this all year: new chain, new cables, the gears that slip in third. You kneel on the cold cobbles and start.

At eleven the bike is in pieces on a sheet of newspaper and you have chain oil up to your elbows, and Frau Özdemir from the second floor comes down with a cup of tea and a börek on a plate and says "*Urlaub?*" Holiday?

"Sort of."

She looks at the bike in pieces and at you, and nods slowly, as if she understands entirely, and goes back upstairs.

At three you pick Mila up from the Hort. She's surprised to see you. She's never seen you at the Hort gate on a weekday.

"Did someone throw you out of the gate?" she says.

"Yes," you say. "I asked them to."

Mila considers this, very seriously, with her satchel on. "Did someone catch you?"

You think about Sanne, on the kitchen floor, *we'll do it*. About Noor, *Thursday? The Späti*. About Jonas, whom you barely know, eating Streuselkuchen at a desk he still has. About Ines in Hamburg and Priya in Hamburg and Dorota at the bakery and Mehmet in Ludwigsfelde and the name of every one of them on a sheet of A4 by a Smart Fridge.

"Yes," you say. "Lots of people."

"Good," says Mila, and holds your hand across Hermannstraße, where the drivers never stop, all the way home.

:: s14-exit
"Last first day," Sanne says, at the Bialetti, looking at you over it. "At Kontor, anyway."

"Last first day."

You gave notice on the fourth. Three months. Your last day is the thirty-first of March. Then Köln, two days a week, the 06:51 ICE, the cathedral at the end of the bridge, a man writing your name in a book.

Sanne puts on the green coat. "Don't let them give you anything new," she says.

"They can't. I'm leaving."

"They'll try," says Sanne, and kisses you on the side of the head, and goes to Wedding.

At the office, the badge reader works first time. The lift is out of order. On the fourth floor, the long room with the arched windows and the six Focus Pods and the empty space where the kicker table used to be, is a third full. Eleven people in Money Movement where there were fifteen. Noor at the desk opposite yours, senior now on the reconciler, by the knowledge list, with a new notebook. Tobi's desk empty, cleared, wiped. A new person at Ines's old desk, a young engineer from the transfer company's retraining programme, placed here by Fatma's Sozialplan, who looks terrified, and whom Noor is already teaching camt.053 to, slowly, from the beginning, not letting her skip ahead.

You sit down. You open the reconciler. You have three months to finish it — properly, the migration, every last test — and to give the rest of what's in your head to Noor and the new one, out loud, slowly, in front of witnesses.

You look at the empty folder from Priya on your desk. MONEY MOVEMENT. *Not twice. Promise?* You weren't the one holding it. You'll leave it for whoever is.

At 09:45, Lars stands at the window by the third arch and reads Otto's summary. No emoji. No sentiment. No *missing updates*.

On the whiteboard on wheels, pushed against the pillar with the sign that says not to lean on it, someone has written under **RAIFFEISEN: 22**, in green marker: **23**. Ines would be pleased. Ines will be, when Noor sends her the photo.

You think: three months. Then very boring. Then a book at the door with your name in it.

You think: that'll do.

:: s14-orchestrator
You take Mila to school, and then you take the U-Bahn, because Directors have a meeting at 09:00 and can't arrive with chain oil on their trousers.

The office. The badge reader. The stairs. The fourth floor: eleven people in Money Movement where there were fifteen, and an operating review at 10:00 in Müggelsee, and your calendar, which you look at on the U8 between Boddinstraße and Hermannplatz.

```calendar Monday 11 January
09:00–09:30 Directors' sync (Rebecca)
09:45–10:00 Money Movement standup (Otto summary)
10:00–11:00 Operating review
11:00–12:00 Works council: Otto Shadow under §87(1) Nr. 6 (Fatma, Sasha, Dr. Kessler)
13:00–14:00 Agent workflow review: reconciliation, dunning, card retries
14:00–15:00 1:1 Noor (senior, reconciliation)
15:30–16:30 Q1 planning: "Money Movement at 11"
```

*Agent workflow review.* That's the new thing. Since December, the agents write most of the code in Money Movement and the eleven people review what the agents write, and you review what the eleven people approve, and every Monday at 13:00 you sit with the dashboards and decide which workflows are reliable enough to run unsupervised. Reconciliation: not yet. Noor says not yet. You trust Noor. Dunning: yes, since November, since C took over Tobi's role and wrote the Burned rules into it. Card retries: Ben says yes. Ben's usually right.

You're good at it. Everyone says so. Rebecca says so in your H2 review, in January, in writing. *Jakob holds the system and the people at once.* You don't write code anymore. You haven't since the reconciler migration, which you finished on the eighteenth of December, with your own hands, as Rebecca asked, the night before the Christmas party; the last commit is yours, and Noor reviewed it.

At lunch you eat a hummus wrap from the Smart Fridge at your desk, because there's no time to go anywhere, and because there's nowhere to go. *Hallo Jakob! Guten Appetit!*

At 14:00 you have your 1:1 with Noor. In a Focus Pod, because Krumme Lanke is a storage room now. Knees touching in the grey felt box. She has her new notebook. She asks you about the reconciler, and you realise halfway through the answer that she knows it better than you do now, and that you're answering from memory, from a year ago, and she's answering from last Tuesday.

"You don't need me for this any more," you say.

"No," says Noor. She doesn't say it unkindly. "But I need you for the other thing." She taps the calendar on her laptop. *Q1 planning: "Money Movement at 11".* "In the room. Holding the folder." She looks at you. "Someone who knows what the reconciler does at three in the morning should be the one who decides who's woken up by it."

That evening, after Mila's asleep, you open Priya's folder at the kitchen table. Inside there are now four things. The scenario page from October. The Interessenausgleich summary from December. The list of the nine, which you copied by hand from the noticeboard, names, not roles. And a sheet of A4 with Q1's plan, which says *Money Movement at 11*, and nothing else, yet.

Inside the flap, in Priya's pencil: *Not twice. Promise?*

You haven't had to do it twice. Not yet. You hold the folder for a long time in the light from the cooker hood, which is the only light on.

:: s14-council
At 11:00 there's a works council meeting in Wannsee, the room with the window and the plant that someone waters, which the council has booked for Tuesdays and Thursdays and, this week, Monday, because the Otto Shadow consent form is up for co-determination under paragraph eighty-seven, section one, number six, and Fatma wants it torn up and rewritten before February.

You're third from the left at the table. Fatma at the head, with the binder. Sasha beside her, with the yellow goalie rod in his back pocket. Dr. Kessler, on Meet, from Schöneberg, with her silver bob and her soft precise voice. Ben, for what used to be Liste 2, who now arrives at every council meeting early and reads every page.

Across the table: Rebecca. Dr. Albers. Lars. The Otto Shadow consent form on the TV, version 1.2, with its *derived knowledge may be retained indefinitely* and its *cannot be withdrawn*.

"Clause five," says Fatma, taking her glasses off. "Withdrawal. *Derived knowledge already incorporated into Otto cannot be withdrawn.*" She looks across the table. "We'd like to talk about that."

It takes three hours. It'll take three more sessions. You don't win everything. You win attribution, which Rebecca had already given in July, if you asked for it, and now in writing for everyone. You win a deletion right for raw recordings after twelve months, not twenty-four. You win a clause that says *recordings and derived knowledge shall not be used in performance evaluation or selection for redundancy, individually or in aggregate* — the point Rebecca wouldn't give you in July, in Wannsee, with the plant. She gives it now, on the record, with Dr. Albers writing down that he advised against it. You don't win *cannot be withdrawn*. Nobody knows how to take something out of a mind once it's in. Not even the people who built the mind.

At the end, Rebecca stays behind for a moment as the others file out.

"You were right," she says. "In July. Point two." She closes her notebook. "It just took a council to make it true."

You're protected now. Paragraph fifteen. For four years and one after. You're also tired in a way you weren't before. People stop you at the Brita tap to ask about their contracts, their parental leave, whether the transfer company counts as continuous service, whether they should sign the new Otto form. You don't get much time on the reconciler. Noor's the senior on it now, by the knowledge list; she has her own new notebook and a new junior from the transfer company to teach, and she asks you things less and less, and you notice, and you're proud, and it's both.

At six you cycle home along the canal in the dark, silent, and the swans are asleep on the bank with their heads under their wings, and you think about your father on the phone from Bad Wildungen: *the works council doesn't stop anything. It just makes them say it out loud, slowly, in front of witnesses.*

They said it out loud. Slowly. You were a witness.

@end The Council

:: s14-burnout
You don't go up.

You stand at the bottom of the stairs, with your hand on the green iron handrail, and you can't. It's not dramatic. You just stand there, and your hand on the rail is shaking, very slightly, the way it shook in March after the refunds went out, and your heart is going like 03:12, and you think about the fourth floor, and the review queue, and the submarine noise, and Lattice, and the Smart Fridge saying your name, and Mila's face in the Aula on the twenty-second of August looking at an empty chair, and you can't put your foot on the first step.

You stand there for eleven minutes. People pass you, going up. Two say *Morgen*. One asks if you're all right. You say yes.

Then you go back down the ramp to the bike cellar, and unlock the Hercules, and ride home along the canal in the drizzle, and call your GP from the courtyard, a woman in Schillerkiez who's known you since Mila was born, and she sees you at three, and listens for twenty minutes, and writes you a *Krankschreibung*: three weeks, to start with. The note says nothing about why. In Germany the note never says why.

You email Lars. Or Rebecca. Two lines. You don't open Slack. You put the phone in the drawer of the bedside table, with the hand cream and the broken earphones and your father's birthday card from last year, and close the drawer.

That was the deal. It was always the deal.

On Wednesday, with Mila at school and Sanne in Wedding, you walk to Tempelhofer Feld. Through the gate at Oderstraße, onto the old airfield, three hundred and fifty hectares of nothing, the runways still there, the terminal like a long grey cliff on the far side, the sky enormous. It's January. Brown grass. Puddles. A cold wind straight from Poland. It's almost empty. A man flying a kite alone. A woman with a dog. Two runners.

You walk the whole length of the main runway, two kilometres, slowly, and back. It takes an hour. Nobody pages you. The sky doesn't care.

At the café by the Columbiadamm gate, the converted shipping container, you buy a hot chocolate for three-fifty and sit on a bench in the wind and drink it. On the next bench there's an old man feeding sparrows from a paper bag. He looks at you. He's seen a lot of men on that bench on weekday mornings in January, you suspect.

"*Krank?*" he says. Ill?

You think about it.

"*Müde*," you say. Tired.

"*Ach*," says the old man, and nods, and throws the sparrows another handful, as if that were the most ordinary thing in the world, which, in Berlin, in January, this year, perhaps it is.

That evening Sanne comes home and finds you on the kitchen floor, with your back against the cupboard, and sits down beside you without a word, and passes you a fork, and a container of börek from Frau Özdemir.

"Tell me something boring," she says.

You tell her about the sparrows.

@end The Burnout

:: s14-engineer
You go up.

The fourth floor: eleven people in Money Movement where there were fifteen. Noor at the desk opposite yours, senior on the reconciler now, by the knowledge list, with a new notebook. Tobi's desk empty. A new person at Ines's old desk, a young engineer from the transfer company's retraining programme, placed here by the Sozialplan, who looks terrified, and whom Noor is already teaching camt.053 to, slowly, from the beginning, not letting her skip ahead.

At 09:45 Lars stands at the window by the third arch and reads Otto's summary. No emoji. No sentiment. No *missing updates*. Eleven people in a crescent. A gap at the end by the arch where nobody stands, about the width of one person, which nobody fills and nobody mentions.

You're still the knowledge. Paragraph one, section three, Anlage 3, row one: *Senior Backend Engineer (reconciliation)*. Seven years, a child, a reconciler, a woman who said yes on a kitchen floor or is still waiting, depending. You're protected by the law and by a list, and you know exactly why, and you know who wasn't, and their names are on a sheet of A4 by the Smart Fridge that nobody has taken down.

You open the reconciler. The migration's done; you finished it in December. What's left is the six per cent. The things the bank won't tell you. The ten-second pause. You've written most of it down. Some of it is still in your head. Some of it, you think, will always be. You've stopped minding.

At eleven, at the Brita tap, Sasha comes past with the yellow goalie rod in his back pocket.

"So," he says. "January."

"January."

"Same as last year?"

You think about it. The radiator. The drizzle. The orange stripe on the ceiling. Mila's knees on your stomach. *Parts of their brains.*

"No," you say. "Not the same."

"No," Sasha agrees. He fills a glass. "Kharkiv, 2015, after. Also not the same." He drinks. "Better, worse, I couldn't tell you. Just not the same." He puts the glass in the dishwasher, which only he and Tobi and you ever do. "That's how you know it happened."

He goes up to the roof.

You go back to your desk. Noor looks up. "There's a Volksbank file with a new namespace," she says. "Again. Version 11." She almost smiles. "Do you want to pause before the obvious fix, or shall I?"

"You," you say. "You're better at it."

"I know," says Noor, and opens it, and pauses, and you watch her pause, ten seconds, doing nothing, looking at a line, and you think it's the most important thing anyone will do on the fourth floor all day, and that it won't show up as anything at all.

@end The Engineer
