:: s4-open
The memo arrives at 08:00 on Wednesday the first of April, which is a mistake that somebody in Communications will be thinking about for a long time.

You read it on the U-Bahn, or at the lights on Kottbusser Damm with one foot on the kerb, or at the kitchen table with Mila's cereal going soft beside your laptop, depending on the morning. It doesn't matter where. Everyone reads it in the same ten minutes, and in those ten minutes the whole company has the same thought, which is: *is this a joke?*

```email
From: Rebecca Lindqvist
To: all-product-engineering@kontor.de
Cc: Christoph Ahrens
Subject: Flattening our organisation: Product & Engineering from today

Team,

Since January we've changed how we build. Today we're changing how we're organised to match.

**What's changing**

- We're moving from 11 teams to 4 **Product Areas**: *Money Movement* (Settlements, Payments Platform, Invoicing payments), *Customer Experience*, *Platform & AI Enablement*, and *Data*.
- Each Area is led by a **Director** reporting to me. Directors: **Priya Raman** (Money Movement), **Paula Schmitt** (Customer Experience), **Lars Becker** (Platform & AI Enablement), **Aylin Demir** (Data).
- The **VP Engineering** role is retired. **Jens Kühn** becomes **Head of Engineering Excellence**, focusing on how we build with AI across the organisation. Jens has been central to everything Kontor engineering is, and I'm very glad he'll keep shaping it.
- Engineering Managers become **Tech Lead Managers**: hands-on, with larger spans and less process. Several managers will move back to individual contributor roles at their own request; they'll share their own news.
- Rituals are simplified: one standup and one retro per Area per fortnight; planning quarterly.

**What isn't changing**

Nobody's role is being eliminated as part of this change. Compensation is unaffected.

**Why**

We have more leverage per engineer than ever. Our structure was designed for a company that solved problems by adding people. We now solve problems differently. Fewer layers, faster decisions, closer to the work.

I'll host an open Q&A in the Kantine at 16:00 today.

Rebecca
```

*Nobody's role is being eliminated as part of this change.* You read that sentence three times. It's precise. *As part of this change.* It's the most precise sentence in the memo, and it's the one everybody on the fourth floor will spend the day taking apart.

:: s4-memo-day
The fourth floor on the first of April behaves like an anthill that someone has poked with a stick: everyone moving, nobody going anywhere.

People stand at the Marzocco longer than usual. People walk to the window and look at the river with their coffee and walk back. People book Krumme Lanke and come out twenty minutes later with blotchy faces. Ben from Invoicing — Invoicing, which no longer exists as a team, which is now *Invoicing payments, part of Money Movement* and *Invoicing experience, part of Customer Experience*, depending on which half of it you were in — stands in the kitchen holding his phone and reading the memo again, out loud, to anyone who comes in, in a flat voice, as if it were a weather report from a country he's never visited.

At 10:40 Priya comes to your desk. She looks like someone who hasn't slept since Thursday, which she probably hasn't.

"Walk?"

You walk. Not to the Späti this time; just down the fire stairs and round the courtyard, twice, in the thin April sunlight, past the bike racks — which are filling up again, now the weather's turned — and the bins and the smokers.

"I'm sorry I couldn't tell you," she says.

"I know."

"I found out three weeks ago. After the incident. Rebecca asked me in a 1:1 if I'd take it. I said I'd think about it. I thought about it for a whole weekend." She laughs. "Then I said yes, because if I said no it would be somebody else, and I didn't know who, and I couldn't stand not knowing who."

"Congratulations," you say, and mean it, mostly.

"Thank you." She doesn't sound like she wants to be congratulated. "Fourteen people. Plus Ben's team. Plus Lars's people on the gateway side. I've got Tech Lead Managers under me who were my peers last week. Ben's old manager, Simone, she's going back to IC. Her choice. *At her own request.*" She says the phrase in the memo's voice. "It's actually true. She asked. She said she's not going to manage people through what's coming."

You stop walking.

"What's coming, Priya?"

She stops too. She looks at the bike racks for a long moment.

"I don't know," she says. "I genuinely don't know. That's the truth. I'm a Director and I don't know." She turns to you. "But I know what *flat* means, Jakob. It means fewer managers. And then it means fewer of the people managers manage. That's what flat always means. First you take out the floor between, and then—" She stops. "Anyway. I'm going to fight for this team. I want you to know that. Whatever happens. I'm going to fight."

"I know you will."

"There's one more thing." She starts walking again, and you fall in beside her. "Rebecca says you owe her an answer. About the documentation project."

:: s4-easter
Before that, though, there's Easter.

Good Friday is the third, Easter Monday the sixth: four days, the first long weekend of the year, and for six weeks there has been a question hanging over your kitchen table that nobody has been willing to answer. Sanne's parents want you all in Utrecht. They always want you in Utrecht at Easter. Her mother hides chocolate eggs in the garden of their terraced house in Lombok and makes a *paasbrood* with almond paste in the middle, and her father takes Mila to see the lambs at the city farm, and it's lovely, and you've gone every year since Mila was born.

And your father in Kassel, on the back of a postcard of the Herkules, wrote *Only if you have time*, which means *please*.

"Go to Kassel," Sanne says, on the Monday before, at the kitchen table, not looking up from the stack of lesson plans she's marking. "Take Mila. Or don't take Mila. Or we all go to Kassel and I tell my mother. I don't care. I mean I do care, but I don't care *which*. I just want you to decide, because if I decide, it's my fault."

Sanne has her own reasons for wanting four days off. Her school in Wedding lost two more teachers in March — one to burnout, one to a better-paid job in Brandenburg — and the headteacher, a gentle exhausted man called Herr Okonkwo, has asked Sanne to be acting deputy head from the summer. Unpaid, for the first year. *Kommissarisch.* She's been carrying the question round for a fortnight, and she hasn't said yes or no, and she hasn't really talked about it, and you've noticed that you haven't really asked.

"What do you want?" you say.

She looks up. "About Easter?"

"About anything."

She looks at you for a long moment over the lesson plans. Then she says: "I want four days where nobody needs anything from me." She almost laughs. "Which is not on the list of options."

:: s4-kassel
You go to Kassel. Sanne goes to Utrecht. You take Mila.

It's the first time you've split a holiday, and at the station on Friday morning, at the bottom of the escalators at Hauptbahnhof, Sanne hugs you for longer than she needs to and says, "Thank you," and you're not entirely sure what for — for taking Mila, or for deciding, or for letting her have four days where nobody needs anything from her. Then she gets on the ICE to Amsterdam with a book and no laptop, and you get on the ICE to Kassel-Wilhelmshöhe with a six-year-old and a rucksack full of snacks.

Your father lives in the flat on the Wilhelmshöher Allee that he and your mother bought in 1987. Third floor, no lift. He's waiting at the top of the stairs when you arrive, holding the door open, leaning on the *Wanderstock*, and he's thinner than at Christmas, and he's put on a tie.

"Mila," he says. "*Meine Güte*. You're enormous."

"I'm normal-sized," says Mila. "You're small."

He laughs so hard he has to sit down on the stairs.

For four days you do the things you did as a child. You walk — slowly, with breaks on benches — up the Bergpark to see the Herkules, the great copper giant on top of his octagon, from below, because the stairs to the top are too much for your father now and he refuses to let you see him take the lift. You eat *Ahle Wurscht* and potato salad on the balcony. Mila draws an axolotl for him and he puts it on the fridge next to a photograph of your mother in 1979, in flared jeans, laughing at something outside the frame.

On Saturday evening, after Mila's asleep in your old room, he pours two glasses of *Apfelwein* and sits down across from you at the kitchen table where you did your homework for thirteen years.

"So," he says. "Tell me what's really happening at work."

You tell him. More than you've told anyone except Sasha. Otto. The incident. The memo. *Flat*. *Nobody's role is being eliminated as part of this change.*

He listens the way he used to listen to the radio news — completely still, frowning slightly, his hands flat on the table.

"In 1993," he says, when you've finished, "Volkswagen had the crisis. You remember? No, you were eight. They wanted to cut thirty thousand people. And instead the works council and IG Metall negotiated the four-day week. Everybody worked less and earned less, and nobody was fired." He turns his glass. "It was terrible. Your mother hated it. We had less money for two years. We didn't go on holiday." He looks at you. "And everybody kept their job. Everybody on my line. Everybody in Baunatal."

"It's different now."

"It's always different now," says your father. "That's what they said in 1993 too." He drinks. "At Kontor. Do you have a works council?"

"No."

He looks at you for a long time, the way he looked at your school reports.

"Then who's going to negotiate for you, Jakob," he says, not unkindly, "when it comes?"

You don't have an answer. He doesn't seem to expect one. He gets up, slowly, using the table, and rinses his glass at the sink, and says goodnight, and you hear him in the hallway, step, stick, step, step, stick, all the way to his bedroom door.

:: s4-queue
The rest of April is the review queue.

You and Sasha are `@kontor/payment-path-reviewers`. Every pull request touching `debit-gateway`, `dunning-service`, `invoice-core/payments` or `reconciler` needs one of you. In the week of the thirteenth there are a hundred and forty-one of them. Ninety-seven were opened by otto-bot.

They come in at night, mostly. You wake up and there are twenty. You go to standup and there are twenty-six. You review four, carefully, and by lunch there are thirty-one. It's like bailing a boat with a cup. Sasha calls it *the tide*. He's started reviewing them on his phone on the U-Bahn in the mornings, which he says he swore in 2016 he would never do, and which he now does with an expression of pure misery, thumb-scrolling through Kotlin diffs between Warschauer Straße and Schlesisches Tor.

The PRs aren't bad. That's the thing. Most of them are fine. Dependency bumps, test coverage, log-line improvements, small refactors. About one in twenty has something wrong with it. About one in fifty has something wrong with it that matters. The problem isn't finding the one in fifty. The problem is that to find it you have to read the other forty-nine, and reading the other forty-nine takes all day, every day, and the tide comes in again overnight.

So you start doing what everyone does under a tide. You triage. You approve the dependency bumps with a glance. You approve the test-coverage PRs if they don't touch production code. You read the refactors properly. You leave anything touching idempotency, timeouts or money amounts until you've had coffee.

And you start, for the first time in your career, closing pull requests without reviewing them. *Closing: not needed. Please don't reopen.* Otto opens them again the next night, slightly different. You close them again. After a week Lars adds a rule that stops otto-bot from reopening a PR a human has closed, and Sasha sends you a single :pray: in DM, his second emoji of the year.

On the Thursday — Thursday the sixteenth — Tobi sits in on the reconciler session for the first time. Whether it's a standing slot or something you've arranged for the day, he arrives at Krumme Lanke with a notebook. A paper one. New, from the stationery shop on Oranienstraße, a black Leuchtturm with the price sticker still on the back.

He doesn't open Otto once in two hours. At the end he looks like a man who's run a half marathon.

"You are fast," says Noor.

"No. I was fast at *typing*." He looks at his notebook, three pages of untidy handwriting with a diagram of a bank statement that looks like a sea creature. "This is the slow bit. I skipped the slow bit."

That evening, at the Jens farewell, he tells everyone at the Settlements table about the camt.053 status codes, at length, with enormous enthusiasm, and Noor sits beside him with her Tupperware and listens to him explain to Ben and Felix what she explained to him four hours earlier, and doesn't correct him once, even though he gets PDNG slightly wrong.

They'll offer them on the day, in Krumme Lanke and Wannsee and every phone booth, one after another, fifteen minutes each. Sign by Friday, get more money. Don't sign, and—" he shrugs his big slow shrug "—maybe they do it the hard way, maybe not. There's no works council, so there's nobody to make them do it slowly."

You sit on the grass. Mila is laughing somewhere, very high and clear.

"Who?" you say.

"Lars doesn't know names. Or he says he doesn't." Sasha drinks. "But you can do arithmetic, Lenz. Ines can do arithmetic. Fatma has been doing arithmetic since February." He looks at you. "Who is on the X-ray? Who is not? Who is two-and-a-half people? Who costs, according to Marta, half of one bug?"

You don't answer. On the far side of the square the bubble man lifts his sticks, and the biggest bubble of the afternoon comes out of the loop, slowly, slowly, enormous, shimmering, and hangs in the air above the crowd for an impossible length of time, and a hundred children reach up for it, and it drifts up and over the trees and doesn't burst, and keeps going, over the rooftops, towards the river, until you can't see it anymore.

"Beautiful," says Sasha. "Doesn't last." He gets up, slowly, a big man getting up off grass. "Enjoy the day, Lenz. It's Labour Day. Ha." He looks down at you. "In Ukraine when I was a boy we had parades for it. Tanks. Very big tanks. Now here we have bubbles." He seems to think about this. "I prefer the bubbles. But I notice who has the tanks."

He goes back to Olena and Danylo. You watch them walk away across Mariannenplatz, three people, a family you didn't know existed, the boy a head shorter than his father and walking exactly like him.

Sanne comes and sits back down beside you. "Who was that?"

"Sasha. From work."

"The one who smokes on the roof."

"Yes."

"He seems nice." She looks at your face. "What did he say?"

You look at the square. At Mila, chasing a bubble that's already burst, still running, laughing, not knowing it's gone.

"I'll tell you tonight," you say.

You do. You tell her everything, sitting on the kitchen floor at eleven at night with your backs against the cupboards, the way you sat in January after the first all-hands. Nine per cent. The twelfth. *Aufhebungsverträge*. Fifteen minutes in Krumme Lanke. Sanne listens with her eyes closed and her head back against the cupboard door, and when you've finished she's quiet for a long time.

"Are you on it?" she says. "Do you think?"

"I don't know."

"Do you want to be?"

You've never thought about it like that. You think about it now, on the kitchen floor. About the severance money. About a summer with Mila. About fixing the bike. About the reconciler, and Noor, and Tobi with his new notebook, and the thirty-one scars, and Jens's A2 diagram on top of the wardrobe.

"No," you say. "I don't think so."

"Then don't be," says Sanne, as if it were that simple, and maybe for one night it is. She takes your hand on the cold kitchen floor. "And if you are, then we'll do it. We've done harder things." She thinks. "Probably."

Outside, Kreuzberg is still going: a sound system somewhere towards Kottbusser Tor, a police siren, someone singing badly in the courtyard. The first of May turning into the second.

Eleven days.
