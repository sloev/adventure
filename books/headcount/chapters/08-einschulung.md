:: s8-open
On Monday the third of August, Rebecca asks you to Wannsee.

You know what it is. Priya told you on the roof in July; you've had three weeks to think about it, and you haven't, not properly, the way you don't think properly about a dentist's appointment. You've thought about it in pieces. On the bike, which is silent now, so there's nothing to drown out the thinking. In the shower. At three in the morning on the Sparkasse night, at the kitchen table, waiting for Herr Wolff to call back. You've thought: *Director.* And then you've thought about something else.

The plant on the windowsill in Wannsee is in its new pot and has grown so much it's touching the glass. Rebecca is there first, with the notebook and the water. She doesn't make small talk. She never does.

"You know why you're here."

"Priya told me."

"I asked her to." Rebecca folds her hands. "I'd like you to be Director of Money Movement from the first of October. Fifteen people. The operating review on Mondays. The budget. The roadmap. The decisions." She pauses. "And, if it comes to it in the autumn, the room. With People. With the council, if there's a council. With the criteria."

She says it plainly, the way she said everything in February. You appreciate it, and it makes it worse.

"Why me?"

"Because you're the person in Money Movement everyone listens to," says Rebecca. "Because you know the systems better than anyone except Sasha, and Sasha would rather be dead in a ditch in Kharkiv — he told me that himself, in those words. Because you asked the right questions all year, sometimes out loud, sometimes not. Because you've been in the room at three in the morning." She pauses. "And because if it isn't you, it'll be someone from outside who's never been woken up by the reconciler, and I think that would be worse for your team. I think you think so too."

You sit in Wannsee and look at the plant.

"I won't pretend it's only a promotion," Rebecca says. "You'll stop writing code. Mostly. You'll stop being the knowledge and start being the person who decides what to do with it. Including—" she chooses the words "—including what happens to the people who have it, if the numbers in Q3 are what I think they'll be. I'd rather you were in that room than anyone else I can think of." She opens the notebook, then closes it again. "Take two weeks. You're on holiday from Saturday, Priya tells me. Usedom. Tell me when you're back."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll respect it," says Rebecca. "And I'll hire from outside. And you'll keep doing what you do, which is valuable, and which — I'll be honest — is exactly the kind of knowledge the law protects." A very small pause. "Either way, you'll be fine, Jakob. That's not why I'm asking. I'm asking because of who else won't be."

:: s8-usedom
On Saturday the eighth you drive to Usedom.

You don't have a car. You borrow the Özdemirs' — an old silver Škoda estate that smells of child seats and the Turkish bakery — and pack it with three bags, a beach tent, the blue Schultüte, unfinished, which Mila refuses to leave behind in case someone steals it, and a laptop in a sleeve that Sanne looks at in the hallway for a long time before saying, "On call?"

"Not till the twenty-first."

"So why is it coming?"

"In case."

Sanne doesn't say anything. She takes the laptop out of your hand and puts it back on the hall table, next to the post. Then she looks at you. "In case of what, Jakob?"

You think about Rebecca. About Director. About the election lists, due on the eighth of September. About Knowledge Capture, at seventy-four per cent, and Lars asking about the remaining twenty-six. About Noor, on call this week and next, alone with the reconciler, the gateway and the PSP for the first time ever.

"In case of nothing," you say, and leave it on the hall table.

The drive north takes three hours: out through the ring of Plattenbau estates on the edge of the city, past fields of rapeseed already cut, past wind turbines in long lines like enormous patient people waiting to cross a road, onto the A11 towards Szczecin and then off it, north, through Brandenburg villages with cobbled streets and storks' nests on the church towers, across the bridge at Wolgast onto the island. Mila counts storks. She counts fourteen. She's outraged that you didn't see any of them, because you were driving.

The holiday flat is in Ückeritz, a village of thatched houses and pine forest between the Baltic and the lagoon, two hundred metres from the beach. It has two bedrooms, a balcony, a kitchen with a stove that only works on two rings, and no Wi-Fi, which the listing described as *a chance to disconnect* and which Sanne reads out to you in the car park in a voice of pure triumph.

For the first three days you don't think about Kontor at all.

The Baltic in August is grey-green and cold and shallow, with sandbanks you can walk out on for fifty metres, and a beach of fine white sand that squeaks under your feet, lined with *Strandkörbe*, the roofed wicker beach chairs you rent by the day. Mila swims. Mila swims every hour of every day, in a pink swimsuit, shrieking at the cold, digging for amber in the tideline (she finds three pieces, two of which are glass), building a sandcastle with a moat that she insists is a home for axolotls. Sanne reads. She reads three novels in three days, lying in the Strandkorb with her sunglasses on, and you realise you haven't seen her read a book for pleasure since February. You lie in the sand and fall asleep in the afternoons like a child, and wake up with the imprint of a towel on your face, and nobody pages you, and the sea goes on making its noise.

On the fourth evening, after Mila's asleep, you and Sanne sit on the balcony with a bottle of Rotkäppchen sparkling wine and the pine forest dark around you, the sea audible through the trees.

"So," says Sanne. "Director."

You'd told her in the car. She'd said *hm* and looked out of the window for twenty kilometres.

"What do you think?" you say.

Sanne takes a long time to answer.

"I think you'd be good at it," she says. "I think you'd be very good at it. I think you'd be the kind of Director who fights for people and loses sleep and stands in the room so someone worse doesn't." She drinks. "And I think in a year you'd be Priya. And I'd be the wife of Priya. Except I'm not your wife." She smiles slightly. "Which is maybe the thing we should decide first."

You look at her.

"Not tonight," she says quickly. "I'm just saying. We've been together twelve years and we've never decided anything that wasn't an emergency." She puts her feet up on the balcony rail. "Director is a decision, not an emergency. So is getting married. So is whether you stand for the works council. So is whether you take your laptop on holiday." She looks at you sideways. "I'd like us to try deciding something. Before it decides us."

:: s8-decide
On the last night on Usedom, Friday the fourteenth, you walk down to the beach alone at ten o'clock.

It's not dark yet; August on the Baltic stays light until nearly half past nine and grey-blue for an hour after. The beach is empty except for a couple at the far end with a small fire, and a man with a metal detector walking slowly along the tideline, waving his wand like someone dowsing for water. The Strandkörbe are locked up for the night with their wooden shutters down, rows of them, like little houses. The sea is very calm. The sandbanks are just under the surface, pale stripes in the dark water.

You stand at the edge of the water with your shoes in your hand.

You think about the room. Krumme Lanke. Wannsee. Karin Sommer with her folder. A spreadsheet with columns. Priya on the stairs in May. Simone at lunch in April. *At least it's not my handwriting.*

You think about the other room. The one with a council across the table. Fatma's list. *Out loud, slowly, in front of witnesses.*

You think about the reconciler, and Noor's thread on Tuesday night, and the fact that she didn't need you, and the fact that that's exactly what you've been working for since January, and the fact that it means, under any criteria, under paragraph one, section three, that she might be about to become the knowledge — and that you might be about to stop being it.

You think about Sanne on the balcony. *I'd like us to try deciding something. Before it decides us.*

The man with the metal detector has reached you. He stops, and nods, and says in a thick Mecklenburg accent, "*Nix heute. Nur Kronkorken.*" Nothing today. Just bottle caps. And moves on.

You go back up to the flat. Sanne is on the balcony with the last of the Rotkäppchen. You sit down beside her, and tell her what you've decided, and why, and she listens with her feet up on the rail and the pine forest dark around her, and when you've finished she says, "Okay," and then, "Thank you for telling me first," and then nothing for a long time.

:: s8-oncall
From Friday the twenty-first you're on call. Primary. Noor is secondary.

It wasn't supposed to be like that. The rota was made in June, before anybody had looked at a school calendar, and the week of the twenty-first was Sasha's. Then Olena's mother fell — not badly; a wrist, in her flat in Kyiv — and Olena flew to Kraków to meet her sister and arrange things, and Sasha went with her, because, he said, "she says she doesn't need me, which means she needs me." He asked you on the Wednesday, at the Brita tap, with an apology on his face you'd never seen there before. You said yes before he'd finished asking. You didn't check the calendar. You knew what was in it.

Saturday the twenty-second of August. *Einschulung.* Mila's first day of school. Not the first day of lessons; that's Monday. The ceremony. The day of the Schultüte.

"It's one day," you say to Sanne on Thursday night, at the kitchen table, decorating the Schultüte. You're gluing a felt axolotl onto the blue cardboard — pink, with six pink gills, three on each side, like the Fasching headband — while Sanne writes MILA in silver glitter-glue letters down the side. "Saturday morning. The PSP doesn't even run batches on Saturdays. Nothing ever pages on a Saturday morning."

Sanne doesn't look up from the glitter glue. "Nothing ever pages on a Saturday morning," she repeats, in a voice you know very well, the voice she uses when a parent tells her their child would never cheat on a spelling test. "Is that in the runbook?"

"Noor's secondary. If I don't acknowledge in five minutes, it goes to her."

"Then turn it off." Sanne puts down the glue. "On Saturday morning, from nine till twelve. Tell Noor. Tell PagerDuty. You can do that, can't you? Override, or whatever it's called."

You can. There's a button. *Schedule override: Noor Haddad, Sat 22 Aug 09:00–12:00.* It takes ten seconds. You've done it for other people's weddings, other people's funerals, other people's children's births.

You look at the button on your phone. You think about Noor on Tuesday night, alone at three in the morning with a Volksbank namespace, pausing before the obvious fix. The *Einschulungsfeier* is at ten in the Aula of the Grundschule round the corner, the one with the garden and the good Hort and the teacher who does the chickens. The school Mila got into because of a letter Otto wrote in February.

The Aula is a hall from 1912 with a stage at one end, tall windows, a parquet floor scuffed by a hundred years of children, and two hundred folding chairs, all full. Parents in good clothes. Grandparents in better clothes. Older siblings in whatever they could be persuaded into. Little brothers and sisters crawling under the chairs. And at the front, in the first three rows, the sixty new first-graders, in a sea of Schultüten — every colour, every size, cones of cardboard with dinosaurs and unicorns and footballs and princesses and, in one case, a very detailed drawing of the U8 line, and in another, Mila's, a pink felt axolotl with six gills, slightly crooked, and MILA in silver glitter down the side.

She's sitting in the second row between Emre Özdemir, whose Schultüte is a dinosaur, and a girl with red hair whose Schultüte is a hundred centimetres tall and who has already fallen over with it once, in the corridor, and will be on the evening news if Frau Demir from the stationery shop is to be believed.

You're in the fourth row with Sanne and your father and Sanne's parents. Your father is in his suit with his *Wanderstock* between his knees and his hands folded on top of it, very upright, very still, as if he were at a works meeting. Sanne's mother is crying already, before anything has happened. Sanne's father is filming with an iPad held up in front of his face, to the visible fury of the people behind him.

The programme is printed on a sheet of yellow paper. *Begrüßung durch die Schulleiterin. Lied der 2. Klassen: „Wir sind die Kinder aus der Löwenzahnschule". Theaterstück der 3b: „Der kleine Rabe geht zur Schule". Aufruf der neuen Klassen.* Welcome by the headteacher. Song by the second years. The headteacher speaks: a small, warm woman in her sixties, who says that school is a place where everyone is allowed to make mistakes, and the parents laugh, and the children don't understand why.
