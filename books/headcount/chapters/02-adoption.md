February in Berlin is not a month. It's a waiting room.

The sky comes down to the height of the television tower and stays there. The snow that falls on the Thursday is grey by Friday and black by Monday, heaped along the kerbs of Herrfurthstraße in long ridges studded with dog shit and fireworks debris left over from New Year. It's minus four in the mornings. The canal freezes at the edges, and the swans stand on the ice looking betrayed. On the U8 everyone is coughing. At the Kita, a sign goes up on the door with a drawing of a clown: **FASCHING! Freitag 13.2. — Kostüme erwünscht!** Mila announces that she will go as an axolotl, and that this is non-negotiable, and that the costume must have gills, and that the gills must be pink.

"Where do you buy axolotl gills?" Sanne asks you at eleven at night, searching on her phone.

"You don't buy them. You make them. Out of felt."

"Do you know how to make things out of felt?"

"No."

"Neither do I." She keeps scrolling. "Maybe Otto knows."

You look at her.

"It was a *joke*," she says.

At work, the Otto rollout reaches one hundred per cent on the twenty-first of January. Christoph posts about it in `#general` with a GIF of a dog wearing sunglasses. The Looker dashboard on the kitchen TV now cycles through six screens instead of one. The phrase *leverage* starts turning up in places it didn't used to — in Jira ticket descriptions, in Confluence page titles, in the subject line of an email from Facilities about the new recycling bins. Somebody in Sales changes their Slack status to *10x-ing* and nobody can tell if it's a joke.

#4821 goes out to five pilot customers on the twenty-eighth of January, behind its feature flag: a bakery chain in Leipzig, two physiotherapy practices in Hamburg, a scaffolding company outside Graz, and a dog-grooming franchise in Utrecht that Sanne finds funny for reasons she won't explain. Return rates are watched. They're fine. Hanne posts a chart in `#settlements` with a green line that goes gently down, and Christoph reacts to it with :rocket:, and the rocket stays there all week like a flag on a moon.

Rebecca Lindqvist starts on Monday the second of February.

The Otto Champions kickoff is on Tuesday the third of February at 09:00 in Müggelsee, the big meeting room on the south wall, the one with the long table and the good chairs and the view of the river. It's the first meeting Rebecca Lindqvist has ever held at Kontor. She's been here twenty-five hours.

There are fourteen champions — one per engineering team, plus Data and Support — and they arrive the way people arrive at the first meeting with a new boss, which is to say early, and quietly, with coffees, and sit down in a slightly wider spread than necessary around the table so as not to look like they're sitting close to her. You know most of them. Aylin from Data, flat-voiced, already typing. Lars from Platform, who now runs AI Enablement and looks as though he hasn't slept since Christmas. A woman from Invoicing named Paula whom you've only ever met in incident channels. A man from Mobile you've never seen before in your life. And, from Support, Jonte — the young guy with the septum piercing — who looks as though he'd rather be anywhere else.

Rebecca arrives at 08:59. She has a laptop, a notebook — paper, black, A5 — and a bottle of water. No coffee. She sits down not at the head of the table but halfway down one side, which you suspect is a technique, and it works: the whole room subtly reorganises itself around her.

"Good morning. Thank you all for being here, and thank you for volunteering. I know some of you were volunteered." A small ripple of laughter. "I'm going to keep this to forty-five minutes, because I hate meetings that run long and I'm going to be in a great many meetings this month."

She doesn't use slides. That surprises you. She just talks, clearly and without notes, in a voice that doesn't need to be raised.

"Here's what I believe. Tools don't change organisations. Operating rhythms change organisations. You can give everyone a hammer, and in six months you'll have a lot of hammers in drawers. So what I'm asking of you is not enthusiasm. I'm asking for rhythm." She opens the notebook. "Every two weeks, we'll meet in this room. Each of you brings three things: one use case that worked in your team, one that didn't, and one blocker. Not anecdotes — examples. With links."

People write that down.

"Second. We'll be watching the adoption data together. Lars has built a very good dashboard." Lars, at the end of the table, looks up like a man hearing his name in a dream. "I want to be precise about what that data is for. It's for us to understand where enablement is needed. It's a diagnostic." She pauses — that same small pause she did at the all-hands. "Some of you will see individual-level data as part of this group. That's necessary for diagnosis. It's confidential to this group. It does not leave this room."

Around the table, fourteen people do not look at each other.

"Third. By the end of Q1, I'd like every team to have a written answer to one question." She turns the notebook round so you can all see the page, where she has written in neat capitals: **WHAT DOES THIS TEAM DO THAT OTTO CANNOT?** "I don't mean that as a threat," she says, into the silence. "I mean it as a strategy question. If we know what's uniquely human about each team's work, we can invest there. If we don't know, we're just guessing."

Aylin, without looking up from her laptop, says: "And if the answer's 'not much'?"

Rebecca looks at her with what seems like genuine appreciation. "Then we'll have learned something important," she says, "and we'll talk about it honestly. Together."

After the meeting, Lars sends the champions a link. You open it at your desk. It's a Looker dashboard called **Otto Adoption — Champions View (CONFIDENTIAL)**. The top half is teams. The bottom half is a table, one row per person, sortable. Your team, filtered:

Module 1 is a series of videos presented by a man in a black T-shirt standing in front of a whiteboard on which someone has drawn a neural network in four colours. He explains tokens. He explains that the model "predicts the next word", and then explains that this is "a simplification", and then spends eleven minutes explaining the simplification without ever quite getting to what it simplifies. The video player doesn't let you skip forward. You can set it to 1.5×, at which point the man in the T-shirt sounds like he's being chased.

So everyone plays it on their second monitor while doing something else. For a week, the fourth floor is full of a low murmur of men in T-shirts saying *transformer architecture* at one and a half times normal speed, from forty different laptops, slightly out of sync, like a choir in a cathedral where everyone has started at a different moment. At standup on Tuesday, Ines is visibly watching Module 2 on her phone, held below the level of the whiteboard. Priya pretends not to see.

Each module ends with a quiz. You have to score eighty per cent. You can retake it as often as you like.

```doc Kontor Academy · Module 2 Quiz · Question 4 of 10
**A colleague tells you Otto produced code that "looks right but feels wrong". What is the best next step?**

- (a) Trust your instinct and rewrite the code yourself
- (b) Ask Otto to explain its reasoning and refine your prompt with more context

*Correct answer: (b). Great job! Remember: Otto works best when you give it rich context.* :sparkles:
```
