:: s10-open
October comes in grey and wet and smelling of chestnuts. The lindens on Herrfurthstraße drop their leaves all at once in a single windy night, and in the morning the cobbles are yellow, and Mila walks to school through them kicking them up in fountains, satchel bouncing, shouting at the Özdemirs' boy to do the same. The canal is high and brown again. The swans' cygnets are grey and nearly as big as their parents and still following them everywhere, like teenagers who haven't worked out how to leave.

The cheese from Utrecht is finished. The pencil box from 1991 lives in Mila's satchel. The Schultüte stands on top of the wardrobe next to the cardboard tube with Jens's diagram in it, two cones of cardboard keeping each other company.

At Kontor, the first week of October is quiet in the way the air is quiet before a thunderstorm. People talk about the board meeting on the fifteenth without saying *board meeting*. They say *the fifteenth*. In the kitchen, at the Smart Fridge, in the Focus Pods with the doors shut. *After the fifteenth.* *Let's see what happens on the fifteenth.* Ben, at the Brita tap, says he's booked a holiday for the sixteenth "in case I need to cry somewhere warm", and laughs, and nobody else does.

You clear your desk drawer on the Friday. You don't know why. You just do it: old printouts, a dead mouse, three conference lanyards, a Kinder Bueno wrapper from February that must have been Ines's. At the bottom there's a sheet of paper you'd forgotten: your codeword sheet from a different kind of book entirely, a gamebook from 1987 that Sanne found at a flea market and gave you as a joke last Christmas, with a column of pencilled words you'd half-filled in on the sofa. You look at it for a while. Then you take an eraser and rub out some of them, the ones that don't matter any more, to make room.

:: s10-linkedin
The recruiters start on the first.

You've had recruiter messages all your career. Two or three a month, more in spring, fewer at Christmas, the ordinary background noise of being a senior engineer in Berlin. They follow a pattern: *Hi Jakob! I came across your profile and was really impressed by your experience in payments. I'm working with an exciting Series B fintech…* You've answered perhaps four in seven years.

In the first week of October you get thirty-one.

Some of it is the ordinary noise turned up. But a lot of it isn't. A lot of it mentions Kontor by name, which recruiters never used to do, and mentions May, and mentions — in one case, astonishingly — the works council.

```doc LinkedIn · Messages (31 unread)
**Anja K., Talent Partner** · 2d
Hi Jakob, I know things at Kontor have been turbulent since May. If you're open to a confidential chat about a Head of Payments Engineering role with a stable, profitable business (insurance, Köln, hybrid), I'd love to talk.

**Marvin T., Founding Recruiter @ Lumenwerk** · 3d
Hey Jakob 👋 We're building AI agents that do bookkeeping for SMBs end-to-end, incl. bank reconciliation (camt.053 → ledger, fully autonomous). You literally wrote the book on this at Kontor (your reconciler runbook is legendary 😅). Founding Engineer, top of band + equity. Coffee this week?

**Sebastian R., Executive Search** · 4d
Dear Mr Lenz, in light of the recently constituted works council at Kontor, I would like to discuss positions in which your experience of employee representation in a technology environment could be valuable…

**Daniela M., Recruiter @ a large US cloud provider** · 5d
Hi! Your profile came up as a strong match for our AI Agent Reliability team. Since the market is moving so quickly…
```

*Your reconciler runbook is legendary.* You read that one three times. The reconciler runbook is in Kontor's Confluence. It's internal. It's behind a login. It's —

You look up Lumenwerk. Twenty-three people, an office in Mitte, a Series A in June, two founders who used to be at a bank. On the team page, among the engineers, there's a face you recognise: a man from Lars's old gateway team who left in April, before the twelfth of May, before anyone else left. *Founding Engineer, Integrations.*

He knew the runbook existed. He knew what was in it. He's told them.

Or he's told them it's *legendary*, which isn't the same as giving it to them. You don't know. You'll never know. You sit at your desk with the LinkedIn tab open and feel, for the first time this year, the specific vertigo of realising that what's in your head has a price, set by people you've never met, and that the price is quite high.

It's your first operating review as Director of Money Movement. Monday at ten, Müggelsee, Rebecca and the four Directors round the long table, a deck on the TV, and at the end, before *any other business*, an item you weren't expecting:

*Scenario planning for the board (15 Oct): Areas at −10% / −20% / −30%.*

Money Movement's has three columns. *−10%: 13.5 FTE. −20%: 12. −30%: 10.5.* And underneath each column, empty rows. Lines to fill in. You look at the paper. *10.5 FTE.* Fifteen people. Four and a half fewer. You think of the buff folder from Priya, in your bag, empty, with three words in pencil inside the flap.

At your desk afterwards, you take the folder out and put the page in it. The first thing in it. It doesn't look like much. It looks like a form.

:: s10-scenario-lars
Lars finds you at the Brita tap at eleven, interim Director of Money Movement for five days, with the face of a man who has been handed a newborn by a stranger on a station platform.

"Have you got ten minutes?" he says. "I need someone who knows what anything does."

You sit with your knees touching in the grey felt box, door shut, *In use* glowing red outside, and Lars shows you the paper Rebecca handed out at the operating review that morning. "I'll tell you what each system needs," you say slowly. "What breaks at three in the morning and what it takes to fix it. That's all. Not who."

"That's all I need," says Lars, with enormous relief. Then, after a moment: "It's the same thing, though, isn't it."

"Yes," you say. "It's the same thing."

You don't reply. You leave both messages unread, with the little blue dot beside them, and the LinkedIn tab closed.

:: s10-lumenwerk
Lumenwerk's office is on the fourth floor of a new building on Invalidenstraße, opposite the Natural History Museum, all glass and pale wood and a reception desk with nobody behind it, only an iPad that says *Hi! Who are you here to see?* and, when you type *Marvin*, plays a short cheerful sound and says *Marvin's on his way!* There's a dog asleep on a beanbag. There's a kicker table, a new one, unscuffed, with a sign that says *Leonhart Pro Series*.

Marvin is twenty-six, in a hoodie, enthusiastic and kind. He takes you to a meeting room called *camt.053* — "we name all our rooms after standards, it's a thing" — and the two founders join, a man and a woman in their early thirties, both from a bank, both with the particular calm of people who've raised money recently.

They show you the product. It's good. It's genuinely good. An agent that reads a small business's bank statements every night, matches every payment to every invoice, chases the ones that are late, and files the result in the accounting software, without a person touching anything. They show it running on a demo company, a bakery, and it reconciles a month of statements in eleven seconds.

"We're at ninety-four per cent fully autonomous," says the woman founder. "The six per cent is the hard part. Empty reference fields. Pending entries. Banks that send the same booking twice across a weekend." She smiles. "You know."

You know. You know because you've written it down. Forty-one pages, or twenty-two, depending on what you chose in July. *Things the bank won't tell you.* The Volksbank `1234.5`.

"We'd like you to own the six per cent," says the man founder. "Founding Engineer, Reconciliation. Top of band, and two-tenths of a per cent of the company." He names a salary. It's thirty per cent more than you earn. "And honestly — we don't need you to write much code. The agents write the code. We need you to tell them what's true."

*Tell them what's true.* You sit in a room called *camt.053* with the dog snoring next door and feel the full weight of the year arrive in a single sentence. They don't want an engineer. They want the thing Rebecca asked for in April and Lars built in May: the knowledge, separated from the person, bought once, at a good price, and then owned. They're more honest about it than Kontor ever was. They put it in the job title.

"Can I ask you something?" you say. "When the six per cent is done. When the agents know everything I know. What does the Founding Engineer for Reconciliation do then?"

The founders look at each other. It's the first time they've looked at each other in the whole meeting.

"Honestly?" says the woman. "We don't know. We think there'll always be a new six per cent." She pauses. "But we're not going to pretend we know. That's not how we work."

On the way out, Marvin walks you to the lift and says, cheerfully, "By the way, we've also got Tobi Brandt coming in on Monday. From your team? Super excited. Is he as good as everyone says?"

:: s10-koln
On Friday the ninth you take the 06:51 ICE from Berlin Hauptbahnhof to Köln, four and a half hours across the whole flat brown width of northern Germany, through Hannover and Bielefeld — Tobi's country, cows and wind turbines — and into the Ruhr, and out of it again, and across the Rhine on the Hohenzollern bridge with the cathedral standing right there at the end of it, black and enormous, as if someone had parked it.

The insurance company is a twelve-storey tower from 1974 in Deutz, on the other side of the river, with a view of the cathedral from every window that faces west, which everyone who works there mentions within five minutes. The lobby has a security desk with a real person behind it, a man of sixty who looks at your ID card and then at you and then writes your name in a book, by hand, with a pen.

The interview is three people at a round table on the ninth floor. A Head of IT who's been there twenty-two years. A team lead from payments, a woman of about fifty with reading glasses on a cord exactly like Jens's. And a young engineer, the only one under forty, who is there, he says, "to see if you're nice."

They don't ask you to write code. They ask you to tell them about the worst incident you've ever had. You tell them about March. All of it: the timeout path, the idempotency key, the hundred and eighty-seven thousand euros, the refunds, Christoph on the phones, the postmortem. They listen without interrupting for twenty minutes. When you've finished, the woman with the glasses says, "And what did you change afterwards?" And you tell them about Burned, and the thirty-one scars, and *What I didn't do and why*.

The Head of IT writes something on a pad of paper. A real pad, with a pen.

"We have a committee," he says, "evaluating AI coding tools. Since 2024. It meets monthly. It hasn't decided yet." He smiles slightly. "Our board would like it to decide faster. Our works council would like it to decide slower. I would like it to decide correctly." He puts the pen down. "We're going to need someone who's been through it. Someone who knows what happens on the twelfth of March."

*Someone who's been through it.* Priya's recruiter's words, in June. You think of Priya, in Hamburg, in a shipping company, five years behind.

"We'd want you two days a week in Köln," says the woman with the glasses. "Three from Berlin. We pay less than a fintech. We don't have equity. We have a pension, a canteen, and thirty days' holiday, and we haven't fired anybody for operational reasons since 2003." She looks at you over the glasses. "We're very boring. That's the offer."

On the train home, crossing the Rhine at dusk with the cathedral lit up gold behind you, you sit in the quiet carriage and don't open the laptop. You look out of the window at the dark fields and the wind turbines with their red lights blinking in time, and you think about *a canteen*, and *very boring*, and a man writing your name in a book by hand.

:: s10-tobi
On Monday the twelfth, Tobi isn't at his desk in the morning, and you know where he is, if you know about Lumenwerk. If you don't, you find out at four, when he comes back from what his calendar says was a dentist's appointment, and sits down at his desk, and doesn't open his laptop, and after a while comes over and asks if you want to go up to the roof.

It's cold on the roof now. Grey sky. The plastic chairs from 2019 are wet. Tobi doesn't sit. He stands at the parapet with his hands in his hoodie pocket.

"I had an interview," he says. "At a startup. In Mitte. Lumenwerk. They do agents for bookkeeping." He laughs, short. "They want me as an *agent engineer*. Not writing code. Supervising agents that write code. Top of band. Equity." He looks at the river. "Because I was the Otto champion. Because I *ship fast*. It's literally on my LinkedIn. *Shipped 6-week epic in 2 weeks with AI.*" He laughs again. "From January."

"Are you going to take it?"

Tobi doesn't answer for a long time.

"In January I'd have taken it on the spot," he says. "I'd have run there. It's everything I thought I wanted. Fast. Agents. Equity. Founders who say *we don't know* like it's a feature." He takes his hands out of his pockets and puts them on the wet parapet. "And then they showed me their reconciliation agent. And it does camt.053. And I asked them what it does with PDNG entries. And they didn't know. They said *that's the six per cent*." He looks at you. "I knew, man. I knew the answer. Because of you and Noor and four hundred printed pages. I knew more about their product's hardest problem than their founders did." He shakes his head. "And they want to hire me to supervise agents. Not to know things. To *supervise*."

He's quiet for a while. A tourist boat goes past below, nearly empty, a few people in raincoats on the upper deck.

"The calibration sheet," Tobi says. "*Does role need to exist.*" If you know about the note, you know it; if you don't, he tells you now, flatly, the way you'd tell someone a diagnosis. "I keep thinking about that. And here's the thing. Maybe it doesn't. Maybe in Money Movement, at ten and a half people, a Tobi who writes fast and checks slowly is the role that goes. That's — honestly, I get it. I'd cut me too, if I were reading that spreadsheet." He turns round and leans his back against the parapet. "But at Lumenwerk the role is *nothing but* a Tobi from January. Fast. Trusting. Supervising things he doesn't understand." He looks at you. "I don't want to be that guy again. Even for equity."

"So you'll stay."

"I don't know." He pulls his hood up against the drizzle. "Maybe I'll stay and get cut in November. Maybe I'll go and be January-Tobi for twice the money." He almost smiles. "Maybe I'll go and be a carpenter. My uncle in Osnabrück is a carpenter. He says nobody's built an agent that can hang a door yet." He pushes off the parapet. "Thanks for teaching me the slow stuff. It's ruined me for startups."

He goes down the fire stairs. You stay on the roof a while, in the drizzle, looking at the river, where the tourist boat has turned round under the Oberbaum bridge and is going back the way it came, empty.

:: s10-father
On Saturday the seventeenth, two days after the board meeting, at 06:10, the phone rings.

Not your phone. The landline. You have a landline because the internet contract came with one, and in nine years only three people have ever called it: a man selling solar panels, Sanne's mother, once, by mistake, and now this.

It's Frau Brandau, your father's neighbour on the third floor of the Wilhelmshöher Allee, a widow of eighty who has known you since you were eight. She's been trying your mobile, she says, but it's on silent. She heard a noise at five. She has a key. She found him at the bottom of the hall, by the front door, on the floor. Conscious. Cold. He'd been going to the bakery. He'd fallen on the step by the shoe rack, the step that's always been there, that he's stepped over every day since 1987.

"*Die Hüfte*," says Frau Brandau. The hip. "*Der Notarzt sagt, die Hüfte.*" The emergency doctor says it's the hip. "*Sie haben ihn ins Klinikum gebracht. Er hat nach Ihnen gefragt.*" They've taken him to the Klinikum. He asked for you.

You're on the 08:04 ICE to Kassel-Wilhelmshöhe. Sanne drives you to the station in the Özdemirs' Škoda, which they lend without being asked, the moment Sanne knocks at seven in her dressing gown, with Mila asleep in the back in her pyjamas. At the drop-off point at Hauptbahnhof, Sanne gets out and hugs you, hard, in the rain, with the engine running, and says, "Call me from the hospital. Whatever it is." And then: "Jakob. Whatever it is, we'll do it." And Mila, waking up in the back seat, presses her face against the window and waves, and doesn't know why.

The Klinikum Kassel is a hospital from the 1970s on a hill on the edge of the city, with long corridors of pale green lino and a café in the lobby that sells Streuselkuchen. Your father is on the trauma ward, fourth floor, room 412, by the window. He's had surgery at ten: a hip replacement, a *Hüft-TEP*, the surgeon says in the corridor, a young woman with her mask round her neck, very common, very routine at his age, the operation went well. It's the next weeks that matter. The rehabilitation. Whether he can manage the stairs at home. Whether he can manage at home at all.

He's asleep when you go in. Small in the bed. Smaller than at the Einschulung, smaller than at Easter, if you were there. His hands on the blanket, very still, with a cannula taped into the back of one. The *Wanderstock* is leaning against the bedside cabinet. Somebody — Frau Brandau, it must have been, or the paramedics — brought it with him.

You sit beside the bed for two hours. Nurses come and go. A man in the next bed, whose hip is also new, watches football on a small screen with headphones and occasionally says "*Ach, komm!*" Out of the window the hill drops away to the city, grey roofs, the Wilhelmshöhe park in the distance, and on top of it the Herkules, small as a chess piece, green copper against the grey sky.

At three your father opens his eyes.

He looks at the ceiling for a while. Then he turns his head and sees you, and something happens in his face, slowly, like a light coming on in a house down a long road.

"Jakob," he says. His voice is hoarse. "You came."

"Of course I came."

"Busy," he says. "You're busy."

"Not today."

He looks at you for a long time. Then he lifts the hand without the cannula, an inch off the blanket, and you take it. It's cold and dry and much lighter than you remember.

:: s10-father-talk
You stay in Kassel until Monday.

You sleep in your old room on the Wilhelmshöher Allee, under a poster of the 1990 World Cup team that your mother never took down, and in the mornings you climb the three flights of stairs from the street, counting them, *fifty-four*, looking at each one the way the surgeon looked at the X-ray, imagining your father climbing them with a new hip and a *Wanderstock*. You look at the step by the shoe rack. You don't know how he's stepped over it for thirty-nine years.

In the hospital on Sunday, a social worker comes, a calm woman with a folder, and explains the *Pflegegrad*: the care-level assessment, the application to the care insurance, the visit from the medical service in a few weeks to decide how much help he'll get. *Kurzzeitpflege*, short-term care, after the rehab clinic, if he can't go straight home. A form for you to sign as next of kin. You sign it at the café in the lobby, over a Streuselkuchen, and think about Dorota.

On Monday morning, before your train, your father is sitting up, with colour in his face, and has eaten a yoghurt and complained about it.

"Go home," he says. "You've got a little girl and a woman you haven't married yet and a company that's falling apart. Go home."

"Papa—"

"I'll be in the rehab clinic for three weeks. Bad Wildungen. Very nice. Spa town. Old people in dressing gowns everywhere." He almost laughs. "I'll fit in." He looks at you. "And Jakob. The woman."

"Sanne."

"I know her name. I'm old, I'm not stupid." He takes your hand again. "Ask her. Your mother and I were married forty years. The day we got married was the second-best day of my life."

"What was the best?"

"The day you were born," says your father, "and the day you started school with that box, and the day Mila did. I can't choose. It's a draw." He lets go of your hand and waves at the door. "Go. Your train. You'll miss it. You were always late."

:: s10-board
The board meets on Thursday the fifteenth, in Munich, at the offices of the lead investor, from nine until four. Christoph and Rebecca and Marta fly down on Wednesday night. Nobody at Kontor sees the deck.

On the fourth floor, Thursday is the quietest day you can remember. Nobody books Krumme Lanke. Nobody goes to the roof. The Focus Pods are all *Available* for the first time since June. People work, actually work, heads down, headphones on, the way people work in a hospital waiting room: to have something to look at. At four o'clock half the floor checks Slack at the same moment. Nothing. At five, nothing. At six, people go home. You cycle home in the dark along the canal, silent, and Sanne asks at the door, "Anything?", and you say, "Nothing," and she says, "Nothing is something," which is the kind of thing teachers say.

On Friday the sixteenth, at 08:00, there's an email.

```email
From: Christoph Ahrens
To: all@kontor.de
Subject: The next chapter of Kontor

Team,

Yesterday I presented our Q3 results to the board. They're not what we planned. Revenue grew 19% year-on-year, below our 30% target. Our costs, despite everything we did in May, are still too high for the growth we have. And the way we build has changed more this year than in the previous ten.

The board has asked us to reach profitability by Q3 next year. To do that, we will reorganise Product & Engineering around a smaller, more senior core, supported by Otto. **We are proposing to reduce Product & Engineering by up to 31 roles (about 22%).** Some other functions will see smaller changes.

This time it will be done differently, and properly. Kontor now has a works council, and the law gives it the right to be consulted before any change like this. We informed the works council this morning, and we'll begin negotiations on an *Interessenausgleich* and a *Sozialplan* next week. No decisions about individuals have been made, and none will be made until those negotiations are complete. If the outcome includes dismissals, selection will follow the criteria the law requires.

I said in May that I'd stay in the room. I'm going to. There will be a Q&A today at 11:00 in the Kantine, and every week until this is done.

Christoph
```

*Up to thirty-one roles.* *About twenty-two per cent.* *Product & Engineering.*

You read it on the U-Bahn, or at the lights on Kottbusser Damm, or at the kitchen table with Mila's cereal going soft beside your laptop, exactly as you read the memo on the first of April. It doesn't matter where. Everyone reads it in the same ten minutes.

And then, at 08:14, an email from the works council, to all employees.

```email
From: Betriebsrat Kontor Berlin <betriebsrat@kontor.de>
To: berlin@kontor.de
Subject: What happens now

Dear colleagues,

This morning management informed us of a planned *Betriebsänderung*: a reduction of up to 31 roles in Product & Engineering.

What this means:
- **Nothing happens to anyone until the negotiations are finished.** No conversations in small rooms. No agreements to sign by Friday.
- We will negotiate an **Interessenausgleich** (whether, when and how) and a **Sozialplan** (severance, support, criteria).
- If there are dismissals, the **Sozialauswahl** applies: length of service, age, dependants, disability. We will review the criteria and the data. We will not accept "role criticality" or "flight risk" as selection criteria.
- Please **do not sign anything** you are offered individually. Talk to us first.

Our door is open (Wannsee, Tuesdays and Thursdays 12–14, or betriebsrat@kontor.de).

Fatma Yılmaz, for the works council
```

*We will not accept "role criticality" or "flight risk" as selection criteria.* In front of witnesses, cc everybody. The columns are named, in public, by the council, before management has said a word about them.

At 08:30 you get to your desk. Noor is already there. She has her notebook open in front of her. She's written something at the top of a fresh page, in her small careful handwriting, and underlined it twice. You can read it upside down from across the desks.

*31.*
