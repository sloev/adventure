:: s1-alarm
The alarm goes at 06:40, which is a lie you tell yourself every Sunday night. You will not get up at 06:40. You will get up at 06:52, when Mila climbs over Sanne and lands on your stomach with both knees and the full moral authority of a six-year-old who has been awake since six.

"Papa. Papa. It's Monday."

"I know it's Monday."

"Axolotls can regrow their brains."

"Parts of their brains," says Sanne into her pillow. "We talked about this."

"Parts of their brains," Mila agrees, unbothered, and goes off to find her socks, which will take eleven minutes and involve the whole flat.

It is still dark outside. It will be dark until nearly eight. Through the gap in the curtain the streetlight on Herrfurthstraße shows a fine diagonal drizzle that can't commit to being snow. The radiator under the window ticks. You lie there for one more minute doing the thing you have done every first-Monday-back for seven years, which is to run through your calendar in your head and feel the shape of the week settle on your chest like a cat.

You did not look at Slack over the holidays. That was the deal you made with Sanne in the car on the way back from her parents' in Utrecht, somewhere around Hannover, with Mila asleep in the back and the wipers on intermittent: no laptop, no phone in bed, no "just checking". You kept it. Mostly. You looked once, on the 30th, at the `#incidents` channel, the way you'd check that you locked the door. Nothing had burned down. You closed it without reading anything else, and felt virtuous, and then felt stupid for feeling virtuous about not doing your job on a public holiday.

So you don't know what's waiting. That's the thing about the first Monday. Two and a half weeks of other people's messages, stacked up like post behind a door.

The kitchen is too small for three people and always has been. Sanne makes coffee in the Bialetti because the electric kettle is broken and nobody has replaced it; you make Mila's bread with the good butter and cut it into the triangles she insists on and pack an apple she will not eat. The Kita expects a *Frühstücksbox* and has opinions about sugar. Mila's last year there. "You're quiet," Sanne says.

"First day back."

"It's not your first day. It's your seven-hundredth day."

"It's my first day back."

She looks at you over the Bialetti the way she looks at children in her class who say they've done their homework. Then it's 08:10 and you are standing on the pavement with your helmet in your hand and the drizzle in your face, and you have a decision to make that you make every morning between November and March.

The Hercules is locked to the railings in the courtyard. Its rear brake has been making a noise like a dying seagull since October. The ride is twenty-two minutes along the canal. The U8 to Kottbusser Tor and the U1 to Schlesisches Tor is twenty-eight minutes door to door, if nobody is lying on the tracks, and it is warm, and it smells like wet coats.

:: s1-bike
It is not only drizzle.

By Hermannplatz it has become a fine, determined sleet that finds the gap between your collar and your neck with a precision you'd admire in a missile. You go along the canal anyway, because the canal is the good route even in January: the black water, the swans who look like they're being paid to be miserable, the houseboats with their stovepipes smoking, a man in a full Gore-Tex suit doing pull-ups on the bridge at Kottbusser Damm as if he's being filmed. The rear brake screams at every light. A woman with a cargo bike full of two toddlers under a rain cover gives you a look of deep solidarity at the Glogauer Straße crossing. You give her one back.

The office is in the old factory on the Spree side — six storeys of yellow-brown Berlin brick with a chimney they kept for the architecture, KONTOR in white letters on the side of it in a font that a branding agency charged eighty thousand euros to choose in 2021. You remember that number because the CFO at the time mentioned it in an all-hands as an example of something they would never do again. The CFO at the time is now at a crypto company in Lisbon.

You go in through the side courtyard and down the ramp into the bike cellar, which is lit by one fluorescent tube that flickers at a frequency that probably counts as a workplace hazard. It smells of wet rubber and chain oil. There are maybe twenty bikes down here in January, compared to a hundred and twenty in June. The fair-weather cyclists have gone back to the U-Bahn. You lock the Hercules to the rack next to a carbon road bike that costs more than your car would, if you had a car.

"Lenz."

Sasha Kovalenko is in the corner by the drying rack, peeling off a pair of waterproof trousers with the deliberation of a bomb disposal technician. He's forty-six, the size of a wardrobe, and the only other person on the Settlements floor who bikes all winter. He has a beard that has gone grey in a stripe down the middle like a badger. He has been an SRE at Kontor since before it had SREs — when it was "the guy who knows Kubernetes" — and he has been on the pager for longer than anyone in the building.

"Sasha. Good holidays?"

"My mother came from Kharkiv for two weeks." He shakes the trousers out. Water goes everywhere. "So, no."

"How is she?"

"She is very well. She told me I am fat, my apartment is cold, and my German is getting worse. All three are true." He hangs the trousers on the rack, next to yours. "You read Slack yet?"

"No. I did the thing. No laptop."

He makes a sound that might be a laugh. "Then I will not ruin it for you. You will see." He picks up his backpack. "There is a new channel."

"What channel?"

"You will see," he says again, and goes up the stairs two at a time, leaving wet bootprints, and you stand in the flickering cellar with sleet melting down the back of your neck, feeling the first small cold pulse of the year.

:: s1-ubahn
The U8 is warm and smells like wet coats. You get a seat at Boddinstraße, which is a small miracle, and sit with your knees against the knees of a man in a Hertha scarf who is asleep with his mouth open, and you open Slack on your phone for the first time in seventeen days.

The app spins for a long time, as if it too is reluctant.

Then the sidebar fills in, bold channel by bold channel. The red badge on the icon says **147**. That's just the mentions and DMs. Below it, the channels you're in go on and on, white text on purple, unread, unread, unread.

You scroll. You are a professional. You know how to triage. You start with the things that could be on fire.

`#incidents` — nothing since your check on the 30th except a Sev-3 on the 2nd: *Invoice PDF rendering slow for NL customers*, resolved by Lars from Platform in forty minutes with a message that just says *restarted the pods, will investigate properly next week* followed by nothing, because nobody ever investigates properly next week.

`#settlements-alerts` — the bot noise of your own team's services. Mostly the `reconciler` complaining that bank statement files from Sparkasse arrived late over the holidays, which happens every year, because the banks run skeleton crews too. You see Sasha acknowledged them all, at hours that suggest he wasn't really on holiday either.

`#settlements` — your team. Forty-odd messages. You'll read them properly at your desk.

And then, near the top of the sidebar, a channel you don't recognise, because it didn't exist when you left.

`#ai-wins`

The train pulls into Kottbusser Tor. You will see.

:: s1-arrive
The badge reader at the main entrance takes three attempts, as it does every Monday, because it goes into some kind of power-saving coma over the weekend. The lift is out of order. There is a laminated sign on it in German and English: *Außer Betrieb / Out of order — Facility informed* :pray:, with a smiley that someone has given horns in biro. You take the stairs. The stairwell is the one place in the building that still looks like a factory: iron handrails painted green, worn stone steps, a smell of old dust and the faint ghost of something industrial, machine oil from 1910.

Floor four. You push through the fire door and the office opens out in front of you.

It's a big room, a hundred metres end to end, all brick and steel columns and the big arched windows looking out over the Spree and the grey bulk of the Oberbaum bridge. When you joined in 2019 it felt like the future. Long white desk rows with monitors on arms. Little islands of plants — monsteras, mostly, a few dying ficuses — watered by a man from a plant service every Thursday who never speaks to anyone. Acoustic panels in orange felt hung from the ceiling in a pattern meant to look like sound waves, which absorb nothing. Six glass phone booths along the north wall, every one of them already booked, two of them already occupied, a man in one of them pacing in a circle like a fish in a tank. The meeting rooms along the south wall are named after Berlin lakes: Wannsee, Müggelsee, Schlachtensee, Liepnitzsee, and Krumme Lanke, the small one with no window that nobody books unless they are about to cry or fire someone.

It's 08:52 and the floor is maybe a third full. Mondays in January. People are still in the trickle-in phase: coats steaming on the backs of chairs, laptops being unpacked, the gentle chorus of fans spinning up. Somewhere a USB-C dock is chiming connect-disconnect-connect. The air already smells of coffee and somebody's microwaved porridge.

The Settlements corner is by the third arched window, in the row between the Invoicing team and the pillar that has a handwritten sign on it saying *THIS IS NOT A STANDING DESK, PLEASE DO NOT LEAN YOUR LAPTOP*. You have a fixed desk. Not everyone does anymore; most of the floor went to hot-desking in 2023, with an app to book seats that nobody used, but Settlements kept its desks because Priya argued that the team needs to be near each other during incidents, and because Jens, the VP of Engineering, likes Priya and couldn't be bothered to fight about it.

Your desk is as you left it: two monitors, a mechanical keyboard that Sanne says sounds like a typewriter in a war film, a mug that says WORLD'S OKAYEST ENGINEER that Ines gave you in a Secret Santa three years ago, a dead succulent, and a printed page pinned to the felt divider that says, in your own handwriting: **RECONCILER: DO NOT TOUCH CAMT PARSER WITHOUT TALKING TO ME.** You wrote it in 2022 after a contractor touched the camt parser without talking to you.

Noor is already at her desk opposite yours, which doesn't surprise you. She's always here by half past eight. She has her headphones on but not playing anything — you can tell because she takes them off immediately when you arrive.

"Hi! Happy new year."

"Happy new year. Good break?"

"Yes. Quiet. My mother made too much food." She smiles. She has a notebook open beside her keyboard, a real paper one, with a list in very small handwriting. She's ten months into her first job. She's from Aleppo by way of Gaziantep and Bremen and a computer science degree at the TU, and she writes tests for everything, including things that don't need them, and asks questions in DMs because she is too polite to ask them in channels. "Did you see…" she starts, and stops.

"I haven't read anything yet."

"Okay." She looks at her screen, then back at you. "There's a lot."

"There's always a lot after Christmas."

"There's more," she says, and puts her headphones back on.

Tobi's desk, next to hers, is empty, but his laptop is open on it and running something — a terminal is scrolling by itself, lines going past faster than a person could type. Tobi's not here. The terminal just keeps going.

You stand there, holding your wet helmet or your damp scarf, and you consider the two things that pull at you on a Monday morning.

The kitchen is ten metres away, around the corner by the lift shaft. You can hear voices from there already — the morning crowd at the coffee machine, where everyone stands holding their cups for twenty minutes while the information of the building is exchanged. If anything happened while you were gone, it's being discussed there, right now, in a lower voice than it would be on Slack.

Or you could sit down, put your headphones on, and read the 147 messages before anyone has a chance to ask you anything.

:: s1-coffee
The kitchen at Kontor is the size of a small apartment and contains the single most expensive object on the fourth floor: a two-group La Marzocco espresso machine, bought in 2021 when the company raised its Series C and was spending money on things as an expression of identity. Everyone calls it *the Marzocco*. It has a laminated sign of its own — *Please clean the steam wand after use!!! This means YOU* — and on Mondays it is broken. Nobody knows why it is broken on Mondays. There is a theory that the cleaners turn it off at the wall on Friday nights and something in its Italian soul can't handle the cold start. There is a Jira ticket in the Facilities project, FAC-212, that has been open for nineteen months.

This morning it's half-broken: the left group works, the right group drips and hisses. So there is a queue.

The queue is the point. You join the back of it behind Ben from Invoicing, who is wearing a Christmas jumper either ironically or because his laundry situation has collapsed, and behind him Aylin from Data, and in front of her Lars from Platform, who is holding a cup that says I PAUSED MY GAME TO BE HERE, and at the front Hanne, your product manager, who is steaming oat milk with the focus of a surgeon. "Utrecht. Sanne's parents. You?"

"It's a theory. I don't say it's true." She looks at you. "Have you used Otto?"

Otto. You know about Otto the way you know about the weather in another city. It's the name the company gave its internal AI platform in the autumn — a licence with one of the big vendors, wrapped in a Slack bot and an IDE plugin and a GitHub integration, named after Christoph's Vizsla, which comes into the office on Fridays and eats things off desks. There was a pilot in Customer Support in November. A few teams got access in December. You were in the December batch and you installed the plugin, asked it to explain a forty-line function in the reconciler, got an explanation that was about seventy per cent right, and closed it, because it was the week before Christmas and you had a release to babysit.

"Tobi's been using it non-stop," Ben says. "He was posting at three in the morning. He did the whole retry thing for you guys."

"So I hear."

"Is it good?"

"I haven't seen it."

"It's good," says Hanne, turning round with her flat white. Her hair is shorter than before Christmas. She has the look she always has in January, of someone who has written all her OKRs over the break and is waiting to find out which ones survive contact with leadership. "Morning, Jakob. Happy new year. It's really good, honestly. Tobi demoed it to me on Friday on a call. He's going to show it at standup. It's a big deal for the Q1 roadmap — if we can ship retries in January instead of March, that's, like." She makes a gesture with her free hand that means *a big deal for the Q1 roadmap*.

"It touches the gateway," you say.

"I know."

"And the PSP timeouts."

"I know. That's why you're going to review it." She smiles at you, and it is a real smile, she's not a cynical person; she's just someone who would genuinely like for things to go well and for people to like her and for the roadmap to be green. "Tobi said you were the obvious reviewer. Because you know the reconciler."

Lars has his coffee now and is leaning against the counter, blowing on it. "Has anyone heard about the new CTO?"

There's a small pause, the kind of pause where everyone in a kitchen decides how much they know.

"It's not official," Aylin says.

"Jens told Priya," Ben says. "Priya told Felix. Felix told me. She's from the States. One of the big fintechs. Starts in February."

"What happens to Jens?" you ask. Jens Kühn has been VP Engineering since 2017. He hired you. He is fifty, wears the same black jumper every day, and can still write a decent SQL query, which is more than you can say for most VPs.

"He stays," Ben says. "He reports to her. That's the story."

"That's always the story," Lars says, and leaves with his coffee.

Aylin shrugs. Ben makes a face. Hanne is suddenly very interested in her phone. You think about Jens, who once spent a Saturday with you in this building in 2020 fixing a payment outage while eating cold pizza, and who told you then that the trick to a long career was to stay just interesting enough to keep and just boring enough not to promote.

"Also," Aylin says, more quietly, "Support."

"What about Support?"

"The pilot. Otto's been answering tickets since November. Fatma's team." She lowers her voice further, which in the kitchen means you all lean in a centimetre. "They got numbers before Christmas. It closed forty per cent of tier-one tickets without a human."

"That's good, isn't it?" Ben says. "That's the boring tickets. Password resets."

"Sure," says Aylin. "It's good."

Nobody says anything for a second. The Marzocco hisses. The steam wand needs cleaning. You think about Fatma's team, who sit at the long table by the window in the Kantine — twenty-odd people who speak five languages between them and who answer the phone when a bakery in Graz can't work out why its invoices have the wrong VAT rate.

"Anyway," says Ben, "the left group's free."

You make your coffee. Double shot, a splash of whatever milk is left. You do not clean the steam wand, because you didn't use it, which is the sort of moral technicality that keeps offices running. Then you go back to your desk with the cup warm in both hands, and you sit down, and you open Slack.
