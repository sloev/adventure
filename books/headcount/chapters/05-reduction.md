:: s5-open
You wake at 05:50, ten minutes before the alarm, which hasn't happened since Mila was a baby.

It's already light. In May Berlin gets light absurdly early: the sky over the courtyard is pale blue at five, and the birds start at four, a whole orchestra of them in the lime tree, as if there were something to celebrate. Sanne is asleep on her side with one arm over her eyes. You lie still for a while, listening to the birds, doing the thing you did on the first Monday in January, running through the day in your head. Except today there's only one item in the calendar that matters, and you don't know what it'll say.

```calendar Tuesday 12 May
09:00–09:45 All-hands: Company update (Kantine, in person: mandatory)
10:00–17:00 [Hold] Do not book
The second entry was added to every calendar in the company last night at 23:40, by the Office of the CEO.
```

*Do not book.*

You get up. You make Mila's breakfast, triangles, the good butter, the apple she won't eat. You make coffee in the Bialetti, because it's an emergency, even though you haven't said so to anyone. Sanne comes into the kitchen at 06:30 in the green dressing gown and looks at the Bialetti on the stove and then at you, and doesn't say anything, and puts her hand on the back of your neck for a moment.

"Text me," she says. "As soon as you know. Whatever it is."

"I will."

"Even if it's nothing."

"Especially if it's nothing."

She has a staff meeting at eight about the twelve red Post-its. She's going to be on her phone under the table the whole time. You both know this.

You take Mila to the Kita. She chatters about the summer party, which is in three weeks and for which she is to be a sunflower, and the hedgehog song, which has been replaced in her affections by a song about a cloud. At the gate she hangs her jacket on the hook under the laminated hedgehog and then — for the first time in about a year — she turns back, and runs to you, and hugs your legs, hard, for no reason, and then runs off again without a word.

You stand at the Kita gate a second too long. You don't know what children know. You suspect it's more than you think.

:: s5-arrive
At 08:20 the office is fuller than you've ever seen it at that hour.

People have come in early. People who never come in early: Felix, with his headphones round his neck instead of on his ears. Ben from Invoicing, in a shirt with a collar. A woman from Marketing you only know from Slack, sitting on the edge of a desk in the kitchen holding a coffee with both hands, not drinking it. The badge reader at the main entrance has a queue. The lift is working, for once, and nobody takes it; everyone takes the stairs, slowly, in a long line, like a procession.

And there are people you don't recognise. Faces from the Vienna sales office, from the Amsterdam team, from the remote people who've lived in their little Meet tiles for years and now exist in three dimensions, standing in the stairwell holding wheeled suitcases, looking lost. *Travel will be reimbursed.* They've been flown in. They've been flown in to be in the room.

On the fourth floor nobody is working. Laptops are open, but nobody's looking at them. People stand in twos and threes by the windows, talking quietly. The Marzocco hisses. Nobody's queueing for it; people just stand near it, as if it were a fire.

Noor is at her desk opposite yours, very upright, her notebook open in front of her with nothing written on the page. When you sit down she looks up.

"Good morning," she says. Then, after a pause: "I didn't sleep."

"Me neither."

"I made a list." She turns the notebook round. It's empty. "Of things I would do. If. And then I couldn't write any of them down."

Tobi's desk is empty. His laptop isn't there. Then you see him, at the window by the third arch, standing with his arms folded, looking at the river, not talking to anyone, in a clean white shirt you've never seen him wear.

At 08:40 Priya comes down the row. She doesn't stop. She just touches your shoulder as she passes, and Noor's, and Ines's — Ines, at the end of the row, with her orange cardigan on although it's warm, typing something very fast — and Sasha's, who's leaning against the pillar with the sign, and Tobi's back at the window. She touches every shoulder in Settlements, one by one, without a word, the way you'd count children on a school trip. Then she goes into Krumme Lanke and closes the door.

You notice that Krumme Lanke has a sheet of paper taped to its glass door, which it has never had before. **Reserved all day.** So does Wannsee. So does Müggelsee. So do two of the phone booths.

At 08:52 people start moving towards the stairs.

:: s5-allhands
The Kantine is set up the way it was in January: tables against the walls, chairs in rows, the salad bar under its black cloth. But there's no stage this time, and no lights going down, and no slide that says **The Year of Leverage**. There's a lectern, and a microphone on a stand, and a TV with nothing on it but the Kontor logo, white on deep blue.

Four hundred people. More than in January. Everyone who could be flown in, flown in. They fill the chairs and then the aisles and then stand along the walls and in the doorways, and still there are more on the stairs. Dorota is behind the till with her arms folded and her red glasses on, not serving anybody, just watching, the way she watched in January. Rüdiger is beside her in his whites.

You find a place standing at the back, against the wall by the trays. Sasha beside you. Noor on your other side. Tobi a little apart, arms still folded. Ines further along with Fatma, the two of them shoulder to shoulder.

At 09:01 Christoph walks up to the lectern.

He isn't wearing the vest. That's the first thing everyone notices. A dark blue shirt, no tie, sleeves down. He's holding a single sheet of paper. He doesn't say *Hi Vienna, hi Amsterdam*. He doesn't make a joke. He puts the paper on the lectern and holds the sides of it with both hands, as if it might fly off.

"Good morning," he says. "Thank you for being here. I know many of you travelled. I'm going to read what I have to say, because I want to get it exactly right, and because I don't trust myself to say it well otherwise."

He reads.

"Today we are reducing the size of Kontor by thirty-eight roles. That's about nine per cent of the company. The affected roles are in Support, Marketing, Sales, Finance, People, Product and Engineering.

"This is my decision. It was made by the leadership team and approved by the board, but it's my decision, and I want you to hear that from me and not from anyone else.

"In January I stood here and said we were not doing layoffs. I believed that when I said it. I was wrong. Since January, how we work has changed faster than I expected. Some work that people did in January, Otto now does. Some roles were built for a company that solved problems by adding people. And our growth this year is not what we planned. I could tell you that one of those things is the reason. The truth is that it's all of them.

"If you are affected, you will receive a calendar invitation in the next forty minutes for a conversation today, with your manager or director and someone from People. In that conversation you'll be offered an agreement to end your employment, with a severance package that is significantly above what the law requires. You don't have to decide today. You will have until Friday. You can bring someone with you. You can take advice. Please take advice.

"If you don't receive an invitation by 10:00, your role is not affected.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry to everyone who will receive an invitation. You built this company. Some of you built it with me in a Hinterhof in Friedrichshain with a ping-pong table and a broken heater. I'm sorry I said what I said in January. I'm sorry for the way I said it."

He stops. He looks up from the paper for the first time. His face is completely white.

"That's all. There won't be questions now. There'll be a Q&A on Thursday, when people have had time. Thank you."

He folds the paper and walks away from the lectern, out through the side door by the kitchen hatch, the one the catering staff use, and it swings shut behind him.

Nobody moves.

Four hundred people stand in the Kantine in total silence. You can hear the fridges humming behind the counter. You can hear a tram on the Schlesische Straße. Somewhere in the room, someone's phone vibrates against a chair, and everyone flinches.

Then, slowly, people start to take out their phones. Not to read Slack. To look at their calendars. Four hundred people, standing in a canteen, staring at their calendars, waiting.

It's 09:08. The invitations will come in the next forty minutes.

:: s5-wait
Nobody goes back upstairs. Not at first.

The Kantine becomes a waiting room. People sit back down on the chairs in rows, or lean against the walls, or stand by the windows, holding their phones face up, the screens lit. Some people talk, in low voices. Most don't. Dorota, behind the counter, without being asked, without saying anything, starts the coffee machines — the big industrial ones the catering company uses, not the Marzocco — and sets out cups, and a tray of the croissants that were meant for the eleven-o'clock delivery, and nobody takes one, and she leaves them there anyway.

At 09:14 the first phone buzzes.

You know it's the first because of the sound everyone around it makes: an intake of breath, small, from twenty people at once, as if the whole room were one animal. It's a man from Sales, by the window, a big cheerful man you've seen at every Christmas party, who sang karaoke in 2023. He looks at his phone. He doesn't move for a second. Then he puts it in his pocket and stands up and walks out of the Kantine without looking at anyone, and the door swings shut, and the room breathes out.

At 09:16, two more. Both in Marketing. They look at each other across the room. One of them laughs, a high, strange laugh, and puts her hand over her mouth.

At 09:19, four at once, in the Support cluster by the window. You watch Fatma's face as her team's phones buzz around her. She doesn't look at her own. She just reaches out, without looking, and puts her hand on the arm of the person beside her, a young man you don't know, who is staring at his phone and shaking.

You look at your own phone. Nothing. You look at it again. Nothing. You hate yourself for the relief, every time, and you look again.

At 09:22 Felix's phone buzzes.

He's sitting three seats along from you, headphones round his neck. He looks at the screen. He reads it for a long time, as if it were long, though it can only be one line. Then he takes his headphones off his neck and puts them on the seat beside him, very carefully, as if they were something that might break, and says, to nobody: "Huh." And then: "Okay." And then he gets up and goes.

At 09:25, Simone. You see her across the room, the silver hair, the shorthand notebook. She looks at her phone and her face does nothing at all. She closes the notebook and puts it in her bag. On the way out she passes you and stops for half a second and says, very quietly, in her dry Leipzig voice: "At least it's not my handwriting." And goes.

At 09:31, Hanne. She's standing by the trays with a cup of Dorota's coffee, and when her phone buzzes she drops the cup. It doesn't break. It bounces on the lino and coffee goes everywhere, over her shoes, over the floor, and she stands there looking down at it, and Dorota comes round the counter with a cloth, without a word, and kneels on the floor at Hanne's feet and wipes it up, and Hanne starts crying, standing up, silently, with her hands at her sides, while Dorota wipes.

And at 09:33, Ines.

She's standing with Fatma by the window. Her phone buzzes. She looks at it, and nods, once, as if it were a test result she'd expected. She puts the phone back in her cardigan pocket. Then she turns to Fatma and says something you can't hear, and Fatma takes both her hands, and holds them, and they stand like that by the window for a moment, two women who have been at Kontor longer than almost anyone in the room.

Then Ines looks across the Kantine, over all the heads, and finds you.

You understand. You stood in Müggelsee in February and said *eighteen minutes versus two days*. You said *it's a better job than two days of clicking*. :: s5-noon
At 10:00 your phone hasn't buzzed.

You look at it at 10:00 exactly, and at 10:01, and at 10:03, and at 10:07, because the clock on the Kantine wall is two minutes fast and you don't trust it, and because you've seen enough deploys go out late to know that *by ten* is a hope, not a promise. At 10:15 Lars, standing by the door with his director's lanyard and the face of a man at a funeral, says to no one in particular, "That's all of them. That was the last one," and you believe him, because Lars can't keep a secret.

You text Sanne. You've written it in your head forty times. In the end it's two words.

Not you. You sit on a plastic chair at the back of the Kantine with the phone in your hand and the relief comes up through you like warm water, and right behind it, holding on to it, something cold and ashamed, because Ines is in Krumme Lanke and Hanne is crying by the trays and the relief doesn't care.

Noor is sitting beside you. She hasn't moved since nine. Her phone is face up on her knee, dark. She's looking at it the way you'd look at a dog that might bite.

"Not me," she says, as if she's checking.

"Not you."

"Not today." She closes her notebook, which still has nothing written in it. "Simone told me about the spreadsheet. In April. *The young ones without children are at the top.*" She looks at you. "I'm the young one without children."

"There wasn't a spreadsheet today, Noor. These were offers. Agreements. Different."

"I know," she says. "Today was different. Next time won't be." She stands up. "Simone said the second time, they have to do it properly. With the law. With the spreadsheet." She picks up her bag. "I'm going to go and sit in the quiet room for a while. On the third floor. Please don't tell anyone."

She goes.

---

Lunch is at twelve, as always. It's Tuesday, so it's *Gulasch mit Spätzle*, or the vegan *Linsen-Dal*, and nobody eats.

That's not quite true. People eat. People always eat. But the Kantine at lunch on the twelfth of May is a room you'll never forget. Every table has gaps in it. Not gaps where people have left; the thirty-eight are mostly still in the building, in conversations, or at their desks packing, or on the roof. Gaps where people don't know whether to sit together. The Support table by the window is half full, and the people at it are the ones who weren't invited, sitting very close together, not talking. The Marketing table is empty except for two people at opposite ends. At the Settlements table — Money Movement, now — there's you and Sasha and Tobi and Noor, back from the quiet room, and Priya's empty place, and Ines's, and Hanne's, and Felix's, who never ate with the team anyway but whose absence today is suddenly enormous.

Dorota serves everyone herself. She takes the trays from the counter and brings them to the tables, which she has never done, which isn't her job, one by one, and puts them down in front of people, and doesn't charge anybody. When she puts yours down she doesn't call you *Herr Fahrrad*. She just puts her hand on your shoulder for a second, and then on Noor's, and goes back for the next tray.

"She knows," says Sasha, watching her.

"Knows what?"

"Her contract." He cuts his Gulasch into precise squares. "The catering contract runs out at the end of June. Rebecca asked her in February when it ended. Remember? *When someone like this asks your name, is not always a good sign.*" He eats. "They're putting in vending machines. And a *smart fridge*. Lars told me. With salads. You scan your badge." He puts his fork down. "Nineteen years Rüdiger has made Schnitzel in a pan."

You look at Dorota, at the till, with her red glasses on their beaded chain, tapping the card reader for nobody, out of habit.

Tobi hasn't said a word since the all-hands. He's eating his Gulasch very slowly, looking at it. Then he says, to the bowl:

"Felix. Felix was faster than me at CSS. Did you know that? Like, way faster. He did the whole invoice editor in 2023 in three weeks." He puts his spoon down. "And I'm here. And I'm the one who made the hundred and eighty-seven thousand euro bug."

"Otto made the bug," says Sasha.

"I approved it. Or I wrote it. Whatever. Either way I'm here and Felix isn't." He looks up, and his face is a mess. "Why am I here?"

Nobody answers. Nobody knows. That's the thing nobody says out loud at the Money Movement table on the twelfth of May: nobody knows why they're here. Not you, not Sasha, not Noor. The criteria, if there were criteria, are in a spreadsheet nobody has seen. You're here the way you're alive after a car crash: not because you deserved it, but because of where you happened to be sitting.

At 15:20 you find Priya in the fire stairwell between the third and fourth floors.

She's sitting on the stairs, on the worn stone, in the green-painted iron stairwell that still smells faintly of 1910, with her laptop shut on her knees and her phone face down on the step beside her. She's done four conversations. Ines at 09:45. Felix at 10:30. Hanne at 11:15. Simone at 13:00. Four times fifteen minutes, in Krumme Lanke, with Karin Sommer from People beside her reading from a script, and a folder on the table with the agreement in it, and the photo of the real lake on the wall.

She doesn't look up when you come down the stairs. You sit down two steps above her.

For a while neither of you says anything. Somewhere below, a door bangs. Someone laughs on the third floor, and stops, as if they've remembered.

"Hanne asked me if it was because of the sticky," Priya says eventually. "*Stop pretending.* She asked me if she was on the list because she wrote that. Because of the retro." Priya laughs, a dreadful little laugh. "And I couldn't tell her. Because I don't know. I gave Rebecca the list of my Area's roles in March, before the retro. I didn't give her names. :: s5-grey
At 17:00 the Slack accounts go grey.

It happens all at once. Not one by one, the way people leave a party, but in a single sweep, a script running somewhere in the People team's systems at 17:00:00 exactly, so that if you happen to be looking at the right channel at the right moment, you see it: thirty-eight avatars, scattered through the member lists and the threads and the DMs of the whole company, lose their colour at the same instant. The little green *active* dot disappears. The photo goes to greyscale. Underneath the name, in small text: *deactivated*.

You happen to be looking at `#settlements`. You see Ines go grey. And Felix. And Hanne. All at once, like lights going off along a corridor.

Before that, between three and five, the farewell messages come. In `#general`, mostly. People write them at their desks, while packing, with their laptops — which have to be handed in at reception by 18:00 — on their knees, on boxes, on the floor. The messages come in one after another, and under each one the reactions pile up, hundreds of them, the same few emojis over and over, like flowers at a roadside.

Simone's message is the one people screenshot. By half past four it's in three other channels. *Ask about the Sperrzeit.* Half the floor has never heard the word. You have, vaguely: the twelve-week block on unemployment benefit that the Arbeitsagentur can impose if you sign an agreement to end your own employment without good reason, because, in the eyes of the state, you chose to become unemployed. A severance agreement signed in a hurry on a Tuesday can cost you three months of Arbeitslosengeld. Most of the thirty-eight don't know that. Simone knew it. Simone knew it because she was at Sachsen LB in 2009.

At 17:20, at reception, there's a queue of people holding laptops. A table has been set up by the badge reader with a man from IT and a clipboard and a stack of cardboard boxes, flat-packed, with the Kontor logo on them, for personal belongings. Someone had to order those. Someone had to order thirty-eight boxes with the logo on them, and know how many to order, and when.

Ines is in the queue. Felix. Hanne, with red eyes, carrying a monstera plant from her desk that's nearly as tall as she is. The man from Sales who sang karaoke in 2023. A woman from Support you recognise, with a box that has a framed photo of a dog sticking out of the top.

Fatma is standing at the end of the queue, not in it, with her arms folded, saying goodbye to each of her people as they reach the table. Nine of them. One by one. She hugs every single one.

When she sees you on the stairs, she doesn't come over. She just looks at you over her glasses, across the reception, and holds up her phone, and taps it once, and mouths: *Neunzehn Uhr.*

Seven o'clock.

Würgeengel is ten minutes along the canal towards Kottbusser Tor. Home is twenty minutes the other way.

You promised Sanne. *Come home on time tonight.* She's been carrying twelve red Post-its and an unpaid job and a six-year-old who knows things, and she asked for one thing today, and it was dinner.
