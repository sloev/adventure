:: s12-open
On Thursday the third of December, at 10:00, in Müggelsee, Rebecca Lindqvist says something on the record that her lawyer would very much rather she didn't.

You hear it in the room, from one side of the table or the other. Or you read it in the council's minutes on Friday, posted at 19:00, in front of witnesses, numbers and positions, no names, the way the law allows. It doesn't matter which. Everyone in Product & Engineering has read it by Saturday. Some people have read it to their partners, on kitchen floors.

```doc Betriebsrat · Protokoll (Auszug) · 03.12.
**Item 1: Anlage 3 (knowledge holders, §1(3) KSchG), Money Movement**
The CTO stated on the record, in answer to the council's question of 5 November:
> *"The company cannot distinguish the specific knowledge of the junior reconciliation role from that of the senior reconciliation role. The documentation, the incident record and the peer reviews show that the junior engineer holds the same knowledge, and in two documented incidents applied it alone. Under §1(3) KSchG, the company proposes to add the role 'Backend Engineer, junior (reconciliation/integrations)' to Anlage 3."*

Counsel for the company (Dr. Albers) noted his advice against this statement for the minutes.
The council accepts the amendment. Anlage 3 (Money Movement) now lists five roles.
```

*Counsel for the company noted his advice against this statement for the minutes.*

You read that line more often than any other. You can see Dr. Albers in his grey suit, writing it down himself, very neatly, so that if anyone at the investor's offices in Munich ever asks why the CTO volunteered to make the company's dismissal list harder to defend, there'll be a record that he told her not to.

And under it: *Anlage 3 (Money Movement) now lists five roles.*

Noor isn't in the social selection any more. She's on the list of people the company has formally declared it can't do without.

:: s12-noor-swap
Which means — you work it out at your desk on Friday morning, the way you'd work out a race condition, slowly, with a pencil — that Noor didn't need the Ringtausch. Rebecca's statement would have saved her anyway. Your role, freed on the twenty-seventh of November at 11:43, goes by the swap to the next person on the list in Money Movement: C, the gateway engineer, already saved by Tobi's departure, isn't eligible twice. So it goes to the next lowest: a backend engineer from the payments platform called Jonas Weber, thirty, no children, eighteen months at Kontor, whom you've spoken to perhaps four times in incident channels, who was going to be dismissed in January and now isn't.

You signed so that Noor could stay. Noor would have stayed anyway. Jonas stays because you signed.

You sit at your desk with the minutes open and you don't know, for a long moment, whether to laugh. It's the most Kontor thing that's happened all year. You did a good thing for the wrong person, and it was still a good thing, and the right person was saved by a Swede in a grey office in Munich remembering a mainframe programmer called Bengt.

Noor comes to your desk at eleven. She's read it too.

"I didn't need it," she says. "Your signature. Rebecca said it."

"I know."

"But you didn't know that on the twenty-seventh." She looks at you for a long time. "You signed without knowing." She opens her notebook and shows you a page. At the top it says *27.11. Ringtausch*, and under it *Thursdays until January*, and under that, in fresh ink, today's date and one word, underlined twice: *Jonas.* "I went to find him this morning," she says. "At his desk. I told him who signed. He didn't know. He cried." She closes the notebook. "He's going to bring you a cake. I told him you like Streuselkuchen. I don't know if you do."

"I like Streuselkuchen."

"Good," says Noor. "Then it's all right."

:: s12-noor-list
You read it at your desk on Friday morning, twice. Then you look across the desks.

Noor has read it too. She's sitting very still with her headphones round her neck and the minutes on her screen and her notebook closed in front of her, both hands flat on top of it, the way she held it on the twenty-seventh at 12:04.

You don't say anything. She doesn't either. After a long time she opens the notebook and writes something, small and careful, and underlines it twice, and then she gets up and goes to the Smart Fridge and comes back with two coffees, one with milk, which she puts down on your desk without a word, the way Mehmet did in March, and goes back to her chair and puts her headphones on.

At four, when you go to the kitchen, she's there at the Brita tap, and she says, without looking at you: "Your four pages. Dr. Kessler read from them. In the session. On Thursday." If you wrote them. If you didn't, she says instead: "My peer review. Dr. Kessler read from it. Somebody wrote it as a case." Or: "The incident reports. The Volksbank night. She had all of them in a folder." Whichever is true. "Rebecca said it. But someone gave her the words."

She fills her glass.

"I'm going to learn everything that isn't in the runbook," she says. "Before I'm the senior. So that when someone is twenty-four in five years, I can write it down for them."

:: s12-numbers
The rest of the Interessenausgleich takes another week.

It happens the way these things happen in Germany, which is to say slowly, in a room, with lawyers, over coffee from the Smart Fridge's machine, which nobody has learned to love. Six more sessions. Eleven drafts. Jannik's slides, revised, with the council's alternative now costed down to the euro. The economist from Munich that the company hires to rebut Jannik's slides, who turns out to agree with two of them. Marta the CFO, on a Meet tile from Vienna, saying *I could live with that* about a number nobody expected her to live with. Christoph, who isn't on the negotiating team, coming down to Müggelsee at the end of every session at seven or eight in the evening, standing in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, not saying anything, just there, *in the room*, the way he promised. Sasha, according to the minutes, speaking only twice in six sessions, both times in English, both times about on-call rotas, both times changing the outcome.

On Thursday the tenth, at 23:40, Fatma and Rebecca sign it.

Nine.

In October it was thirty-one. In the council's alternative, in November, it was zero. Nine is what it costs to meet in the middle, in Germany, in 2026, at a company that elected a works council in September and has a CTO who remembers Bengt.

It's nine people. They have names. They'll find out on Monday.

:: s12-hearing
Before a single dismissal can be delivered, the works council must be heard. Paragraph one hundred and two. Each dismissal, individually: the company sends the council the name, the reasons, the social data, and the council has a week to object. Without the hearing, the dismissal is void.

:: s12-hearing-council
So on Friday the eleventh, from 09:00 until 15:00, you sit in Wannsee with the eight other council members and read nine files.

They're not thick. Two pages each. A name. A date of birth. A start date. Children. The comparable group. The points. The reasons, in Dr. Albers's grey prose. Fatma reads each one aloud, in full, because the law requires that the council *deliberate*, and you can't deliberate on a page you haven't heard.

You know four of the nine. A woman from Customer Experience who sat at the Support table by the window in January and laughed at Fatma's jokes. A data engineer Aylin hired in 2024. A man from Lars's AI Enablement team, one of the three engineers who built the Otto Shadow app, which nobody remarks on and everyone notices. And Marco's deputy from Customer Experience, who stood up at the works meeting in June and asked a question about part-time contracts.

The other five you don't know at all. Five people who've worked in the same building as you for one or two or three years, whose names you've seen in Slack and never put a face to. That's the thing that stays with you afterwards. Not the four you know. The five you don't.

The council objects to two of the nine, under paragraph one hundred and two, section three: one because a comparable role in another Area was not offered first, one because a severely disabled employee's status was not correctly weighted in the points. Dr. Kessler drafts the objections. The other seven the council does not object to, because there is nothing in the law to object with. Fatma signs the forms. Her hand is perfectly steady. When she's finished, she takes her glasses off and puts them on her head and looks out of the window at the river for a long time, and nobody says anything.

Sasha, beside you, has the yellow goalie rod on the table in front of him. He's been turning it in his hands all day.

"Nine," he says, finally, quietly. "In Kharkiv it was everybody." He puts the rod down. "That's not nothing, Lenz. Nine is not nothing." He looks at the rod. "I don't know if it's good. But it's not nothing."

You're not on the council, so you don't see the files. On Friday evening Sasha tells you about them on the roof, in the cold, without names, the way he's allowed to.

"Nine files," he says. "Two pages each. Fatma reads every one aloud, every word, because the law says *deliberate*. Six hours." He smokes. "Two we object to. Mistakes in the points. The rest — nothing in the law." He flicks ash into the coffee tin. "I knew four. Five I didn't know." He's quiet for a while. "That's what stays. The five I didn't know. Two, three years in the same building."

He takes the yellow goalie rod out of his back pocket and turns it in his hands.

"Nine," he says. "In Kharkiv it was everybody." He puts the rod back. "That's not nothing, Lenz. I don't know if it's good. But it's not nothing."

:: s12-monday
On Monday the fourteenth of December, the letters are delivered.

Not in Krumme Lanke this time. Not fifteen-minute slots. The Sozialplan forbids it. Each of the nine receives a call from their Director at 09:00 asking if they're in the office or at home; then the letter, on paper, signed by Christoph in blue ink, delivered by hand by Karin Sommer, with the council's representative present if the employee wants one, which all nine do. Each letter comes with a copy of the Sozialplan, a severance calculation to the cent, the transfer company's brochure, and a printed sheet with the council's office hours and Dr. Kessler's phone number.

It takes all day. By evening, nine Slack avatars have not gone grey, because the Sozialplan forbids that too: accounts stay active until the thirty-first of January, *to allow colleagues to say goodbye properly*. Fatma negotiated that line herself, at 22:30 on the tenth, the last thing on her list.

At 16:00, Tobi isn't at his desk.

You find him in a Focus Pod. Number four, by the north windows, where the beanbags used to be. The screen outside says *In use* in red. Through the glass door you can see him sitting on the little stool inside with his hood up and his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands.

You knock. He doesn't look up. You open the door anyway and squeeze in, knees touching in the grey felt box, door shut, the hum of the ventilation, the absurd silence of the acoustic panels.

"Jonas Weber," Tobi says, without lifting his head. Or, if you didn't sign: "Lukas from payments platform." Either way, a name. "He sat next to me at the summer party. On the roof. He told me about his girlfriend in Leipzig. He was going to move there in spring." Tobi's voice is very flat. "He's on the list, or he isn't, depending on who signed what, and I signed, and I'm going to Lumenwerk for twice the money, and I'm the one who caused March." He takes his hands away from his face. His eyes are red. "How is that the arithmetic? How is that *fair*?"

You don't say anything. There isn't anything. You sit in the Focus Pod with him, knees touching, in the hum.

"My dad," Tobi says, after a long time. "When the lorry came for the cows. He did the accounts after. That night. At the kitchen table. I was eleven. I watched him." He wipes his face with his sleeve. "He wrote down every cow's name. In the book. Next to the price. All of them. I asked him why, they're gone, why write the names. And he said, *because somebody should*."

He looks at you.

"Somebody should write their names down," he says. "The nine. Not the roles. The names."

"Fatma has them. The council has them."

"I know." Tobi pulls his hood down. "I mean us. Somewhere a person reads."

On Wednesday the sixteenth, Lars posts in `#ai-enablement`. It's a public channel. Three hundred people are in it. Thirty-one read it in the first minute.

```slack #ai-enablement

From February, Otto Shadow and Knowledge Capture tooling will be maintained by Platform core, and the AI Enablement team will be 3 people instead of 6. Three colleagues who built most of what you use every day are leaving us. I'm not going to name them here without asking them; they'll say goodbye in their own way. But I want to say: the source attribution on every Otto answer, the "what I didn't verify" line, the thing that blurs your screen when you're recording, the human-fallback trigger on the Support phone line: they built those. Thank you. :pray:
~ :pray: 203  :heart: 141  :broken_heart: 37
Aylin Demir [11:09]: The team that built the tool that captured everyone's knowledge so it wouldn't leave with them. Leaving. I'm not making a point. I just want somebody to have written it down.
~ :100: 88  :eyes: 41
```

Nobody replies to Aylin. Nobody needs to. It sits in the channel all week, with its eighty-eight :100:s, like a sticky note on a board saying *Stop pretending*, slightly larger than the others, alone.

:: s12-names
On Friday the eighteenth, the morning of the Christmas party, there's a sheet of A4 on the noticeboard by the Smart Fridge, next to the Sozialplan summary and the works council's office hours.

Nobody knows who put it there. It's handwritten, in black marker, in capitals, in a hand you don't recognise and then, a moment later, do: large, slightly wild, a programmer's handwriting, the handwriting of someone who writes notes in Leuchtturm notebooks now and never used to.

At the top it says: *WIR SCHREIBEN IHRE NAMEN AUF.* We write their names down.

Underneath there are twenty-three names. Not nine. Twenty-three. Everyone who has left Kontor since January, as far as anyone can piece together. The two Support colleagues whose fixed-term contracts weren't renewed on the thirty-first of December last year. The six from Kraków. Jonte. The thirty-eight from the twelfth of May, or as many as could be remembered. The nine from Monday. And, at the bottom, in different pens, added through the morning by different people, more: Dorota. Rüdiger. Ayşe. Maik. Mehmet.

By lunchtime someone has written next to *Ines Carvalho*: *Hamburg. Raiffeisen: 22.* Next to *Felix Wagner*: *invoice editor. He was faster than all of us.* Next to *Gerd*, the karaoke man from Sales: *Christmas 2023: "Dancing Queen". Never forget.* Next to *Simone Richter*: *Sie hat uns das Gesetz erklärt.* She explained the law to us. Next to *Dorota*: *Bäckerei Yıldız, Hermannstraße. Go.*

Christoph stops in front of it at 12:30. He reads it for a long time, all of it, every name and every note. Then he takes a pen out of his shirt pocket and writes something at the bottom, very small, and walks away towards the stairs without looking at anyone.

You go and read what he wrote, after he's gone.

*Ich kenne jetzt alle Namen. C.*

I know all the names now.
