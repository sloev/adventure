:: s9-open
September in Berlin is the best month, and everyone who lives there knows it and nobody who visits believes it. The heat goes. The light stays. The linden trees along Herrfurthstraße turn a tired yellow-green, and the evenings are long and gold and cool enough to sit outside in a jumper with a beer, and the city empties of tourists and fills up again with itself.

Mila is a schoolchild now. She goes to the school round the corner every morning at 07:50 with the satchel that's nearly as big as she is, and comes home at four from the Hort with paint on her hands and new words. *Hausaufgaben*, which she's very proud of. *Pause*, which she says is the best subject. *Frau Kowalczyk*, the teacher with the guitar, whom she quotes constantly, as if she were an oracle. On the first of September she learns to write her name. She writes it everywhere. On the fridge, on the bathroom mirror in toothpaste, on the inside cover of the 1991 pencil box, under JAKOB LENZ, in wobbly pencil: MILA.

And at Kontor, September is Lattice.

:: s9-self
The self-review is due on Friday the eleventh.

It lives in Lattice, the performance tool Kontor bought in 2022, a web app in calming lilac with a progress bar at the top of every form and a small animated confetti burst when you submit anything. The H2 self-review has six sections, one per row of the rubric. *Impact. Craft. Collaboration. Knowledge Capture. AI leverage.* And at the end, *Anything else you'd like your calibration group to know?*

You've written self-reviews every year since 2019. You've always hated them, in the ordinary way everyone hates them: the false modesty, the careful inflation, the strange third-person voice that even the first person takes on. *I drove alignment.* *I partnered closely with.* This year you hate it differently. This year you know what the form is for. Not what it says it's for. What it's for.

You open the Knowledge Capture section. *Describe your contributions to documented, validated knowledge in shared systems.* The text box is empty, with a grey placeholder: *e.g. "Authored runbooks for X, Y, Z; validated N Otto-drafted documents."*

You know what you'll write. The reconciler. The validation tide. Burned. The facts are the facts.

What you're less sure about is the last box.

What you write about Noor tonight could be read in that room. What you write about Tobi too.

:: s9-vote
On Tuesday the twenty-second of September, Kontor elects its first works council.

The polling station is Müggelsee, the big meeting room with the view of the river, emptied of its long table and good chairs. There's a folding table at the door with the electoral register on it, eight pages, and Bettina behind it with a ruler and a pen, checking names and crossing them off one by one. There's a cardboard voting booth borrowed from the Bezirksamt, the kind used in real elections, with a little shelf and a pencil on a string. And there's a ballot box: a real one, grey plastic, sealed with tape, also borrowed from the Bezirksamt, sitting on a chair by the window with the Spree glittering behind it.

The ballot paper is a single sheet. Two boxes. **Liste 1 „Kontor für alle"** with nine names. **Liste 2 „Kontor Zukunft"** with seven. You put a cross in one box. That's all. You don't vote for people; you vote for a list, and seats are divided between the lists in proportion to the votes, by a method Mats explains to anyone who stands still long enough, involving dividing by one, two, three, four, which he calls d'Hondt and Sasha calls "Belgian witchcraft".

People come all day. Not in a rush; in a trickle, between meetings, the way people go to the bathroom. Some come with their teams, in groups, laughing. Some come alone, and stand in the cardboard booth for a long time. Gerd's replacement in Sales comes at nine. Lars comes at ten, as a voter, because the election board decided in the end that interim and area Directors who can't hire and fire independently are not senior executives, which Lars says is "the first good news I've had all year". Christoph doesn't come; he isn't on the register. Rebecca doesn't come; she isn't either. At eleven, Dorota comes, in her bakery apron, on her break, having taken the U-Bahn from Hermannstraße, and Bettina has to explain to her gently that she isn't on the register because she was never employed by Kontor, only by the catering company, and Dorota stands at the folding table for a long moment, and then says "*Ich weiß. Ich wollte nur sehen.*" I know. I just wanted to see. And goes and stands at the window for ten minutes, looking at the ballot box, and leaves.

Noor votes at 08:01, first in line. Tobi votes at 12:30 with a Smart Fridge hummus wrap in one hand. Sasha votes at 17:58, two minutes before the polls close, because, he says, in the Soviet Union people who voted early were informers.

At 18:00 Bettina seals the box. At 18:15, in Müggelsee, with the door open — the count is public; anyone can watch — the election board counts the ballots. Forty people watch. Fatma isn't one of them; she's a candidate, she stays away. You watch, or count, depending on which side of the table you're on.

It takes ninety minutes. Bettina reads each ballot aloud — *Liste eins. Liste eins. Liste zwei. Liste eins. Ungültig.* — and Mats and Petra or you make strokes on two sheets of paper, in fives, the way people have counted votes since there were votes.
