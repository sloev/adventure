:: s13-open
The Christmas party is in the Kantine.

Not a hotel ballroom, as in 2023, with a DJ and a photo booth and an open bar and the man from Sales singing "Dancing Queen". Not a boat on the Spree, as in 2024. Not a rooftop in Prenzlauer Berg, as last year. The Kantine. The room with the shuttered counter and the humming Smart Fridge and the lino floor, on the ground floor of the old factory by the river.

Except that at four o'clock on Friday the eighteenth, the grey roller blind over the counter goes up.

You're there when it happens, because Sasha told you to be, at a quarter to four, with no explanation. About forty people are, by chance or because Sasha told them too. The blind goes up with a long rattle, and behind the counter, in a clean white apron, with her red glasses on their beaded chain, is Dorota.

And beside her, at the flat-top, in his whites, with a mallet in his hand, is Rüdiger.

And Ayşe at the salad bar, which has been uncovered and filled. And Maik, twenty-two, in a chef's jacket, a trainee cook now, at a restaurant in Schöneberg, behind the hatch with a stack of plates.

Nobody says anything for a second. Then somebody laughs, high and surprised, and somebody else claps, and then everybody is clapping, forty people in a room with a lino floor, and Dorota stands behind the counter with her arms folded and her chin up and tears running straight down her face into her collar, and doesn't wipe them.

"*Christoph*," she says, when the clapping stops, by way of explanation, and jerks her head at the door. "*Er hat angerufen. In der Bäckerei. Er hat nach meinem Namen gefragt.*" He called. At the bakery. He asked for me by name. "*Für einen Abend. Mit Geld.*" For one evening. Paid. She sniffs, enormously. "*Schnitzel*," she announces, to the room. "*Heute ist Freitag. Aber heute ist Schnitzel. Ausnahme.*" Today is Friday. But today is Schnitzel. Exception.

Christoph is standing by the door with his hands in his pockets, in the dark blue shirt, no vest. He doesn't come in. He just watches. When people turn round to look at him he looks at the floor, and then goes out into the corridor, and you don't see him again for an hour.

:: s13-party
By six the Kantine is full. Not four hundred, as at the all-hands in January. Maybe two hundred and fifty: everyone left, and a lot of people who aren't.

Because the invitation, sent by the works council and the Office of the CEO jointly — the first thing they've ever signed together apart from the Interessenausgleich — said *Alle, die dieses Jahr bei Kontor waren*: everyone who was at Kontor this year. And they came.

Ines, from Hamburg, on the train, with a new haircut and an insurance company's lanyard she forgot to take off. Felix, with his headphones round his neck. Hanne, with a man she introduces as *my boyfriend, he's in logistics, don't ask*. Simone, with her shorthand notebook in her bag. Priya, from Hamburg too, in a different train carriage from Ines, with her two-year-old asleep in a sling. The karaoke man, Gerd, from Sales, who is immediately asked to sing and immediately refuses and is persuaded within twenty minutes. Jonte, from Bielefeld. Marek, from Kraków, in person for the first time, a big grey-haired man with his dachshund on a lead, who finds you at the Schnitzel queue and says "*Okay, so, we have a small situation*" — and you both laugh so much people turn round. Jens, Head of Engineering Excellence, in the black jumper. Mehmet, in a suit, with his wife, a small round smiling woman who looks at your desk plant on the photo on your phone and nods approvingly. Bettina. Mats. The nine, from Monday, all nine, because Fatma went to each of them personally and asked them to come, and said *you're still employees until the thirty-first of January, and this is your party*.

Rüdiger makes four hundred and six Schnitzel. In a pan. Not the fryer.

Rebecca is there. At the edge of things, the way she always is, with a glass of water. She talks to Dorota for a long time at the till, in English and German and gestures. She talks to Marek. She talks to the man from AI Enablement who built the Otto Shadow blur filter and is leaving on the thirty-first, and you see her shake his hand, and hold it, longer than a handshake, and say something that makes him look at the floor.

At eight, Gerd sings "Dancing Queen". Badly. Magnificently. The Kantine sings it with him, all of it, two hundred and fifty people who've had a terrible year, roaring *You can dance, you can jive*, and Dorota behind the counter conducting with a pair of tongs.

:: s13-ines
At nine, Ines finds you by the window, where the Support table used to be. She has a glass of Sekt and the insurance company's lanyard still round her neck.

"I've got a regression checklist again," she says, without preamble. "Two hundred and forty steps. Hamburg. Mainframe payments. They do releases once a month. It's heaven." She drinks. "They're starting to look at AI. A committee. I'm on it." She smiles, a small, dry, Portuguese smile. "I'm the one who asks what happens when it goes wrong."

You don't know what to say. You say, "I'm glad."

"Are you?" She looks at you over the glass for a long moment.

If you defended her checklist in February, she says: "You argued for me. In Müggelsee. In front of Hanne and everybody. I never thanked you." She touches your arm. "I left you the printout. With the colours. Did you keep it?" And when you say yes, that it's in your drawer, she nods, and says "Good. Give it to Noor, when you go," or "when she's ready," whichever it is, and goes back to the bar.

If you backed the automation, she says: "You said *eighteen minutes versus two days*. In February." She holds your eye. "You were right about the eighteen minutes." A pause. "I was right about the four hundred and eighty." She raises her glass, very slightly. "We were both right. That's the problem with this year. Everyone was right." And she goes back to the bar, and you stand by the window with the river black outside, and realise that it's the closest thing to forgiveness you're going to get, and that it's quite close.

:: s13-sasha
At ten, Sasha finds you on the roof.

You went up for air. The Kantine is hot and loud and full of people who used to work here, and the roof is cold and dark and empty, the strip of gravel and the low parapet and the four plastic chairs from 2019, white with frost. The river is black. The Oberbaum bridge is lit red and yellow. The U1 goes over the top in its little lit box. It's minus three.

Sasha comes up the fire stairs with two bottles of beer and Olena's scarf round his neck and the yellow goalie rod in his back pocket, and stands beside you at the parapet, and hands you a bottle, and lights a cigarette, and offers you one, as he has every time for seven years.

You take it.

He looks at you. He doesn't say anything. He lights it for you, cupping the flame against the wind with his enormous hand, and you smoke it, badly, coughing, for the first time since 2004, on the roof of the old factory, in December, and Sasha laughs so hard he has to hold on to the parapet.

"Seven years," he says, when he can speak. "Seven years I offer. Tonight he takes." He wipes his eyes. "Ha. Now I know it was a bad year."

You stand there smoking in the cold. Below, through the Kantine windows, you can hear Gerd starting "Dancing Queen" for the second time, by popular demand.

"So," says Sasha. "What now, Lenz?"

"Köln offered," you say. "In November. Head of Payments Engineering. Two days a week there. A pension. A canteen. They haven't fired anyone since 2003." You look at the river. "I didn't take the package. So if I go, I resign. Three months' notice. No severance. I'd just — go."

"And?"

"And they want an answer by Christmas."

Sasha doesn't say anything for a long time. He smokes. The U1 goes over the bridge.

"Noor is on the list now," he says finally. "The good list. Paragraph one, section three. She doesn't need you any more, Lenz. Not to protect her." He flicks ash over the parapet. "So the question is only: what do *you* need." He looks at you. "That's a question you have not asked yourself once this year. I watched. Not once."

"I'll take it," you say.

You hadn't known until you said it. That happens sometimes, on roofs, in December, with a cigarette you didn't want.

Sasha nods. "Good," he says. Just that. Then: "Tell Sanne first."

"Sanne already knows."

"Sanne always already knows," says Sasha. "I've met her once. I could tell." He drinks. "Tell her anyway. It's different when you say it."

You call Anja K. on Monday the twenty-first. She says *wunderbar* and that the security man at the door has already been told your name. You give notice on the fourth of January. Three months. Your last day at Kontor will be the thirty-first of March, the anniversary, almost, of the Kraków contract ending, of Marek's *when they start with the contractors, it's because they're practising*.

"I'll stay," you say.

Sasha looks at you. "Why?"

You think about it. Honestly, the way Priya always did. "Because Noor's going to be the senior on the reconciler. And she'll need someone to argue with. And because the council's going to have to fight about Otto Shadow in January, and I want to see it." You drink. Sasha laughs. "Those are terrible reasons," he says. "Very good." He stubs out his cigarette in the coffee tin full of sand. "Tell Sanne."

:: s13-what
You stand on the roof until the cigarette's gone. Then Sasha says, "Come. Dorota will want to say goodbye," and you go down the fire stairs together, him first, two at a time, as he did in January in the bike cellar, leaving wet footprints in the frost.

In the Kantine, Gerd has finished. Somebody has started a playlist from 2010. Mila's hedgehog emoji is on a banner someone printed and hung over the shuttered side of the counter, enormous, with felt-tip spikes: :hedgehog: *FROHE WEIHNACHTEN, KONTOR.*

Dorota is behind the till. When she sees you she comes round the counter, which she has never done in six years, and stands in front of you, small and fierce in her apron, with her red glasses on their chain.

"*Herr Fahrrad*," she says. "*Die Bremse.*"

"I fixed it. In July."

"*Ich weiß. Fatma hat es mir gesagt.*" I know. Fatma told me. She takes your face in both hands, which nobody but Sanne and Mila has done all year, and looks at you over her glasses. "*Aber ich höre Sie trotzdem.*" But I still hear you. "*Jeden Morgen. In meinem Kopf.*" Every morning. In my head. She lets go. "*Das reicht.*" That's enough.

Then she says "*Nächster!*" and turns to Marek, who's behind you with the dachshund, and the dachshund barks, and Dorota gives it a piece of Schnitzel, which is strictly against the rules, and nobody stops her.

:: s13-noor
At midnight, when the Kantine is emptying and Rüdiger has turned off the flat-top for the last time with a click you can hear across the room, Noor finds you by the Smart Fridge.

She's in a dark green dress. She came to a party. She stayed until midnight. You've never seen her at a party past eight.

"I wanted to give you something," she says, and holds out her notebook. The paper one. The one she's had since January, with the small careful handwriting. It's full; the last pages are written right up to the margins.

"I can't take your notebook."

"It's not mine any more. I've started a new one." She shows you, in her bag: a new one, the same kind, blank. "This is the year. Everything. Every standup, every bank, every incident, everything people said. What you said in January about the obvious fix. What Sasha said about Kharkiv. What Ines said about the four hundred and eighty." She puts it in your hands. "I wrote it so I'd learn. I've learned it. Now somebody should have it who was there."

You open it. The first page. *12.01. Headcount: flat.* Underlined twice. And under it, in pencil, smaller, from the first all-hands, in the aisle seats at the back: *Jakob says: either they're lying about the productivity or the headcount.* Or, if you didn't say it, *Sasha says: they believe all of it today. That is worse.* Or nothing; just *headcount: flat*, underlined twice, alone on the page.

You close it. You don't trust yourself to read any more of it in the Kantine.

"Thank you, Noor."

"Thank you," she says, "for Thursdays." Or, if you never had Thursdays: "for January. For saying *good instinct*." She looks at you for a moment longer, the way she looks at a bank statement. Then she does something she's never done in a year: she smiles at you, fully, the whole face, like Mila at the Kita gate.

"*Frohe Weihnachten*, Jakob," she says, and goes out into the cold, buttoning her coat, the new notebook in her bag.
